📌 Pin reference

Pip

🎯 Your mission

Tell a computer chip exactly what to do — and watch it obey.

Your LilEx5 board has a Raspberry Pi Pico on it. The Pico has pins, and each pin has a number. Send power out of the right pin and a light comes on. That is the whole idea, and everything else in this module is built on top of it.

What you will learn

What a GPIO pin is

And why every pin has a number you must get right.

How to send power out

Setting a pin to output and switching it on.

How to leave yourself notes

Comments — lines the Pico ignores but people can read.

How to type, save and run

Using Thonny, or the Wokwi simulator in a browser.

💡 Meet the three LEDs

An LED is a tiny lamp. Your board has three, plus a buzzer. Each one is joined to a different pin, and in your code you always use the GP number.

Here is the same thing as a plain drawing, with the number you type for each one:

PartColourPin you type in code
LED1🔴 Red11
LED2🟡 Yellow12
LED3🟢 Green13
BZ1🔔 Buzzer — not a light14
GP14 is the buzzer, not a light It sits right next to the three LEDs in the list, so it is easy to type by mistake. If your program makes a noise instead of a light, you have used 14 when you wanted 11.

🤔 Why does the red LED get GP11 and not GP14?

The numbers are not chosen by you — they are decided by whoever designed the board and soldered the copper tracks. On the LilEx5 the designer ran a track from the Pico's GP11 pad to the red LED, GP12 to the yellow one, GP13 to the green one, and GP14 to the buzzer.

Your code has to match the copper. If you tell GP14 to switch on, the electricity goes where GP14's track goes — to the buzzer — no matter what you meant.

This is why every board comes with a pinout sheet. Ours is the pin reference. Engineers check it constantly; nobody memorises it.

Board labels are not pin numbers The picture printed on the board uses its own labels, like SW1(11) or SW4(37). Those are positions on the connector. In code you always use the GP number.

🔧 Build the circuit

Wokwi is a free website that pretends to be a real Pico. You build the circuit by dragging parts and drawing wires, then press Play. Nothing can break and nothing can burn out.

1

Start a new project

Go to wokwi.com, click New Project, then choose Raspberry Pi Pico and MicroPython.

2

Find your way around

The screen splits in two: your code on the left, your circuit on the right, and the Serial Monitor underneath.

3

Add the breadboard

In the diagram panel, click the blue + button, search for Breadboard and pick the half-size one.

A breadboard is a block of holes that joins parts together without any soldering. It joins them in a fixed pattern, and the whole trick is knowing what that pattern is.

So there are three kinds of hole:

A stripfive holes in one column, above the gap or below it. All five are joined to each other and to nothing else, so two things pushed into the same strip are connected.
The gapthe wide channel down the middle. It joins nothing. The five holes above it and the five below it are two separate strips.
The railsthe long rows marked + and − along the top and bottom. Each one runs the whole length of the board, so a − hole at one end is joined to a − hole at the other.

One rule follows from all of that, and it is the mistake everybody makes once:

Only the ends count A wire joins what its two ends are pushed into, and nothing else. A jumper can lie straight across a rail on its way somewhere else without joining it.
4

Add the parts

Click + again and add these two. Drag each one onto the breadboard, not onto the empty space beside it:

LEDpick the red one
Resistorclick it, then set its resistance to 330

An LED only works one way round. The two legs are different lengths, and that is how you tell them apart. Work out which is which before you push it in — once it is in the board both legs look the same length:

5

Draw the wires

Click one pin, then click the pin you want to join it to. A wire appears. Press Esc if you start the wrong one.

In words: the LED's long leg (A) goes to GP11. The short leg (C) goes through the resistor, down to the − rail, and the rail goes to GND.

The resistor can sit on either side of the LED — what matters is that it is somewhere in the loop. Here it is on the short-leg side, where it is already pointing at the rail.

⚡ Why the resistor?

On a real board, too much electricity rushes through the LED and burns it out in about a second. The resistor slows the flow down — think of it as a narrow gate in a wide corridor.

Wokwi checks for this too: leave the resistor out and it shows a warning triangle on the LED.

Stuck? Check it against the picture Go back over the diagram above one wire at a time: long leg to GP11; short leg to the resistor, the resistor to the − rail, the rail to GND. Almost every circuit that does not work has one wire on the pad next door.

Do this after Wokwi, when you are ready to see it happen for real.

1

Plug in your Pico

Connect the Pico to your computer with the USB cable, and make sure the board's power switch SW5 is on. A small light on the Pico turns on. Nothing else happens yet — that is normal.

2

Open Thonny and find the Pico

Open Thonny. Look at the bottom-right corner and choose MicroPython (Raspberry Pi Pico). When >>> appears in the Shell, Thonny has found your Pico.

3

Wire the LED

There is nothing to wire. The three LEDs are already joined to pins 11, 12 and 13 inside the LilEx5 board — the same circuit you just built in Wokwi, made permanent in copper.

Three safety rules Never connect a 5V pin to a GP pin — GP pins are 3.3 V only. Never join a power pin to a GND pin. Switch the board off at SW5 before changing any wires.

The grey lines starting with # Those are comments — notes for humans. The Pico ignores everything after a #, so you can write whatever helps you remember. Good programmers use them constantly. Type them if you like, or skip them: the program runs either way.

🚀 Exercise — light up all three

One LED was the warm-up. Now do the whole row.

Task: make the red, yellow and green LEDs all come on at the same time, and leave them on.

💡 Clue 1 — I don't know where to start

You already know how to switch on one LED. Do not throw that away — you are going to do the same thing three times, once for each colour.

Look at the LED table again and write down the three pin numbers before you type anything.

💡 Clue 2 — give each LED its own name

Here is the trap. led is just a nickname, and a nickname can only point at one pin at a time. If you write led three times, the third one wins and the first two are forgotten.

So give each LED a name of its own. Good names say what they are:

red, yellow and green are perfect. Each one needs its own Pin(...) line.

💡 Clue 3 — I have three names, now what?

Switching on is the same as before — .on() — but each nickname needs its own line. Three LEDs, three names, three .on() lines.

Your program will be about nine lines long. That is normal.

No answer on this page There is no answer block here on purpose — work it out from the clues, and check it by looking at the board rather than by reading. Your teacher has the worked answer if the class needs it.

🌟 Going further

Finished early? Try these — no answers given.

  1. Switch on only red and green, leaving yellow dark. Like a broken traffic light.
  2. What happens if you name all three LEDs led instead of three different names? Try it, then explain what you see.
  3. Add red.off() at the very end. Why does it look like the red LED never came on at all?
  4. What do you think Pin(14, Pin.OUT) would do? Try it — but keep your finger near the stop button.

📖 Words to remember

GPIOGeneral Purpose Input/Output — a pin you can control from code.
GP numberThe number the Pico knows a pin by. The one you type in code.
OutputA pin set up to send power out.
LEDLight Emitting Diode. A tiny lamp with a long leg (A) and a short leg (C).
ResistorA part that slows the flow of electricity so the LED survives.
VariableA nickname for something, like led or green.
CommentA note starting with #. The Pico ignores it; people read it.
