🎯 Your mission

Make the board tell you when a button is pressed. A number appears on your screen, and it changes as your finger goes down and comes back up.

Everything you have written so far has gone one way. You decided, the Pico obeyed: switch this on, wait, switch it off. The board had no way of knowing you were even in the room.

This activity turns a pin around. Instead of sending power out, the pin sits there and listens — and your program can ask it, at any moment, what it can hear.

That is only half of it. A pin that listens is useless if you cannot find out what it heard, so this activity also gives you your first way of getting a message out of the Pico and onto your screen.

2. Asking the pin what it can hear

Setting the pin up does not tell you anything by itself. To find out what is happening right now, you ask it:

Code is shown as a picture — turn on JavaScript to see it.

Same shape as on and off: the name of the thing, a dot, and what you want from it. The difference is that on and off were orders, and value is a question. Asking it gives you back a number, and there are only ever two possible answers: 1 or 0.

The empty brackets are not optional They are there for the same reason as in on() and off(). Leave them off and Python will not complain — it will just hand you the question instead of the answer, which is a strange and hard-to-spot kind of wrong.

3. The pull-up — why the pin is never left alone

A pin that is listening has a problem. A button that is not being pressed is joined to nothing at all, and a pin joined to nothing does not read 0 — it reads whatever the last stray bit of electricity in the air happened to leave behind. It flickers. It is useless.

So the pin is given something to hold on to: a resistor quietly joining it up to the board's power. That is a pull-up, and PULL_UP is you asking the Pico to switch on the one it already has inside itself.

The three little bars at the bottom of each picture are the symbol for ground — the same GND you wired the LED back to in Activity 1. It is the bottom of everything electrical, and a pin joined to it reads 0.

🤔 Why does the resistor not just short the power to ground when I press it?

It does join them — but through the resistor, which is deliberately large. Only a tiny trickle of electricity gets through, far too little to do any harm. That is the whole reason the resistor is there rather than a plain wire.

It is the same job the 330 Ω resistor did for the LED in Activity 1: a narrow gate in a wide corridor.

⚠️ The number is backwards

This is the part people get wrong, and it is the whole reason the section above exists. Read it twice.

Everyone expects a pressed button to give 1, because pressed feels like on, and on feels like 1. It does not. A button on this board reads:

It makes sense once you have seen the two pictures above. The pull-up holds the pin up all the time. Your finger is not switching something on — it is pulling the pin down to ground. Pressing does not add anything; it takes something away.

Do not try to fix it There is a strong temptation to make the number come out “the right way round”. Resist it. Every input you meet from here on behaves like this, and in the next activity you will tell the Pico when to act on a value rather than trying to flip it. Learn to read 0 as pressed and you will never think about it again.
A way to remember it 0 means nothing is in the way — the path to ground is open. Your finger closed a gate, and closing the gate is what brought the number down.

Where the answer actually appears

Not on the board. Not in your program. It appears in a panel you have probably been ignoring up to now, because until today nothing ever wrote to it.

Two names, one thing Wokwi calls it the Serial Monitor. Thonny calls it the Shell — the same panel where >>> appears when Thonny finds your Pico. They do the same job: they are where the board talks back. From now on, when a program does not seem to be working, that panel is the first place to look.

Why the loop needs a small wait

You already know sleep. In Activity 2 it was there to slow a blink down so your eye could follow it. Here it is doing something that sounds different and is really the same: without it, the Pico reads the pin and prints the answer millions of times a second, and your screen fills with thousands of identical lines faster than you can scroll.

🔧 Build the circuit

There is real wiring to do this time. Start a new project, or reuse an old one and delete the LED — Activity 1 has the step-by-step for getting a project open if you need reminding.

1

Add the button

Click the blue + in the diagram panel and search for Pushbutton. Add one. That is the only part you need — no LED, and no resistor.

2

Understand the four legs before you wire anything

A pushbutton has four legs, and this catches almost everybody out: they are not four separate connections. Two of them are permanently joined inside the button, and so are the other two. There are really only two connections in there.

So if you wire the two legs on the same bar, you have joined a wire to itself. The button will sit there doing absolutely nothing, and there is no error message to tell you why.

The safe habit, and the one every engineer uses: pick two legs that are diagonally opposite each other. Diagonal legs are always one from each bar.

3

Draw the two wires

One diagonal leg to GP2, the other to GND. That is the whole circuit.

In words: the button's top-left leg goes to GP2, which Wokwi draws as physical pin 4. The diagonally opposite leg goes to the GND on physical pin 8. The other two legs are already joined to those two, so leave them empty — they have nothing to add.

Two sets of numbers, and only one of them goes in your code The big white numbers on the board are the physical pins — where a wire physically goes. The GP names beside them are what you type. GP2 is physical pin 4, and typing 4 would give you GP4, which is a different button altogether. Same trap as Activity 1, where GP11 sits on physical pin 15.
Nothing will look like it is working yet A button has no light in it. Until you have written the program and looked at the Serial Monitor, there is no way to tell a correct circuit from a wrong one — which is exactly why this activity needs print.

Nothing to wire, as usual. SW1, SW2 and SW3 are already joined to GP2, GP3 and GP4 inside the board, with their resistors, in copper.

1

Plug in and power up

USB cable in, power switch SW5 on.

2

Find the Pico in Thonny, and find the Shell

Bottom-right corner of Thonny, choose MicroPython (Raspberry Pi Pico), and wait for >>> in the Shell. That panel is where your numbers are going to appear — make sure you can see it before you run anything.

3

Find SW1 with your finger

Press it once now, before there is any code. Nothing happens, and nothing should — a button on its own does nothing at all until a program is asking about it.

The safety rules have not gone away Never connect a 5V pin to a GP pin — GP pins are 3.3 V only. Never join a power pin to a GND pin. Switch the board off at SW5 before changing any wires.

A column of 1s, five a second, quietly scrolling. Hold SW1 down and they turn into 0s. Let go and they go back to 1.

📖 Words to remember

InputA pin set up to be listened to rather than driven.
OutputA pin set up to send power out. What every activity before this one used.
valueThe question you ask an input pin. The answer is 1 or 0, and nothing else.
Pull-upA resistor holding a pin up at 3.3 V, so it always reads something sensible. The Pico has one built in.
FloatingAn input pin joined to nothing, reading random nonsense. What a pull-up prevents.
Ground · GNDThe bottom of everything electrical. A pin joined to it reads 0.
printSend a message from the Pico to your screen.
Serial Monitor · ShellThe panel where those messages arrive. Wokwi calls it one thing, Thonny the other.
PushbuttonFour legs, two connections. Wire two legs that are diagonally opposite.
