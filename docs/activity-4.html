🎯 Your mission

Hold the button down and the red light comes on. Let go and it goes out. The Pico works out what to do all by itself.

At the end of Activity 3 you could watch a number change on your screen, and almost everybody in the room said the same thing: “can we make it turn the light on?” The answer was “not yet”, because the Pico had no way of deciding anything. It could read. It could report. It could not choose.

That is what this activity adds, and it is the biggest single step in the whole module. Up to now your programs have done exactly the same thing every time they ran. From today they can do one thing or a different thing, depending on what is actually happening in the room.

What you will learn

How to ask a question

if — do the next few lines only when something is true.

What to do otherwise

else — and do these other lines when it is not.

One equals, or two

The difference between putting something into a name and asking whether two things match.

Indenting twice

Four spaces put a line inside the loop. Eight put it inside the decision as well.

You need Activities 1 to 3 first This one assumes you can name an output pin, name an input pin, run a forever-loop, stop one, and read a button. None of that is taught again here. If any of it is shaky, go back to Activity 3 — especially the part about what number a button gives you.

🧭 The new idea — a fork in the road

One idea, and it arrives in two steps. First if. Then, once that makes sense, else.

1. Step one — if

Everything you have written so far has been a straight road. The Pico starts at the top, walks down the lines one after another, and never has a choice about it.

if puts a fork in that road. You write a question. When the Pico reaches the fork it works out whether the answer is yes or no, and takes the matching road.

The question goes in a line that looks like this:

Read it out loud, left to right: if the number the button gives me is the same as zero, then …. The colon on the end is doing the same job it did on the while line in Activity 2 — it means “and here is what to do”, and what to do is the indented lines underneath.

You already know two thirds of that line sw.value() is straight out of Activity 3 — it asks the pin what it can hear. The colon is straight out of Activity 2. The only genuinely new thing in the line is the double equals, and it gets its own section below because it is the trap.

2. Step two — else

With if on its own, only one of the two roads does anything. The other road is empty — the Pico simply walks past and carries on.

That is a problem here. Switching the light on when you press is easy; the trouble is that nothing ever switches it off again. Press once and it stays on for ever.

else fills in the other road: and when the answer is no, do this instead.

The word by itself, with a colon and nothing else on the line:

There is no question after else, and there must not be one. It already knows what it means: every other case. The question was asked once, on the if line, and else is simply the other answer to it.

else never comes first, and never comes alone It has to sit directly underneath an if, at exactly the same distance from the left edge, with the lines that belong to the if in between. An else on its own is an error — Python has no idea what it is the opposite of.

Nothing is being put anywhere. This asks whether the number on the left and the number on the right are the same, and hands back one of two answers: yes or no. It is a question, and a question needs two equals signs.

A sentence that fixes it One equals puts. Two equals asks. Say it out loud before you type the if line, every time, until you stop needing to.

The good news: this one shouts

In Activity 2 the indentation trap was so nasty because a wrongly indented program still runs. It does the wrong thing quietly, and you have to spot it yourself.

This trap is much kinder. Type a single equals in a question and Python refuses to run the program at all:

You get an error before anything happens, and the message names the line:

SyntaxError: invalid syntax

Syntax means the shape of the sentence — the grammar. Python is telling you that what you wrote is not a sentence it knows how to read at all, which is exactly right: you asked it to put something into a question, and a question is not a name.

Where you will actually meet this Almost never on the first try, and almost always at half past ten when you are typing quickly. When a program that worked five minutes ago suddenly refuses to start and the word SyntaxError appears, count the equals signs before you look at anything else.

🔢 Which number means pressed

The second trap, and it is not a new one. It is Activity 3’s trap, arriving at the moment it finally does damage.

The question compares the button’s value with 0, not with 1. Zero is the number a button gives you while you are pressing it. If that still feels backwards, go back and read the section in Activity 3 — the pull-up holds the pin up at 1 all the time, and your finger is what pulls it down.

Compare with 1 by mistake and the program still runs perfectly. There is no error and no warning. It simply does the exact opposite of what you wanted:

This is why Activity 3 refused to fix the number for you A student who has quietly decided that pressing “really” means 1 will write this comparison the wrong way round, get an LED that is on except when they press, and have nothing to argue with. Learning to read 0 as pressed is the whole reason that activity existed.

⇥ Indenting twice

The first time in this module that anything is pushed in twice. Activity 2 taught you four spaces. This adds a second four.

Nothing about the rule has changed: Python still decides what belongs to what by looking at how far each line is pushed in from the left. There are just two levels of belonging now.

Read the picture from the outside in. The two short bars at the very left edge run once and are never seen again. The loop line is at the left edge too, and everything belonging to it is pushed in by four. The question line is one of those, and everything belonging to it is pushed in by four more — eight in total.

Your editor does most of it Type the colon on the end of the if line, press Enter, and both Thonny and Wokwi push the next line in another four spaces for you. Coming back out is the part you have to do yourself: press Backspace once before typing else, so that it lines up with if and not with the lines underneath it.

That last point is where nearly everybody trips. The else has to be at four spaces, level with the if, not at eight. Push it in too far and it is inside the very block it is supposed to be the alternative to:

Python answers with SyntaxError or IndentationError depending on how far out you were. Either way it will not run, which is the kind thing to do.

🔧 Build the circuit

Nothing here is new. This circuit is Activity 3’s button and Activity 1’s LED and resistor living on the same Pico at the same time. If you still have either project open, start from that one and add the missing half.

1

Get the parts on the diagram

Click the blue + and add a Pushbutton, an LED and a Resistor. Set the LED to red and the resistor to 330 in the panel on the right.

2

Draw the four wires

Two for the button and two for the LED. The button rules from Activity 3 still apply: pick two legs that are diagonally opposite, and use no resistor on the button.

In words: one leg of the button goes to GP2 (physical pin 4) and the diagonally opposite leg goes to GND (physical pin 8). The LED’s long leg (A) goes through the 330 Ω resistor to GP11 (physical pin 15), and its short leg (C) goes back to the GND on physical pin 18.

Two sets of numbers, and only one of them goes in your code The big white numbers on the board are the physical pins — where a wire physically goes. The names beside them are what you type. GP2 is physical pin 4 and GP11 is physical pin 15; typing 4 or 15 in your program would give you two completely different pins.
Any GND pad will do There are eight of them and they are all the same piece of metal inside the Pico. Pins 8 and 18 are drawn here only because they are the closest ones to the wires that need them — a tidy diagram, not a rule.

Nothing to wire, as usual. SW1 is already joined to GP2 and LED1 to GP11, inside the board, in copper.

1

Plug in and power up

USB cable in, power switch SW5 on.

2

Find the Pico in Thonny

Bottom-right corner of Thonny, choose MicroPython (Raspberry Pi Pico), and wait for >>> in the Shell.

3

Check you can find both parts with a finger

SW1 is the first of the three small pushbuttons. LED1 is the red one. This program is the first that uses an input and an output at once, so being sure which is which saves a lot of time later.

The safety rules have not gone away Never connect a 5V pin to a GP pin — GP pins are 3.3 V only. Never join a power pin to a GND pin. Switch the board off at SW5 before changing any wires.

🤔 Could I not just hand the button’s value straight to the LED?

Some of you will have spotted that there is a shorter way, and it does work. Leave it alone for now, for two reasons.

The first is that it gives you the light on when the button is up, because of the backwards number — so it needs undoing before it does what you want. The second is that this activity is not really about the light. It is about the Pico being able to choose, and if and else are how it chooses for the rest of the module. There is no short cut for the OLED screen in Activity 6, or for a sensor reading that has gone too high.

Get this shape into your fingers now and the next five activities are much easier.

🚀 Exercise — two decisions at once

One button, one light was one decision. Now make the Pico take two of them, every time round the loop, without either one interfering with the other.

Task: SW1 lights the red LED and SW2 lights the green one:

  1. Hold SW1 and only the red light comes on.
  2. Hold SW2 and only the green light comes on.
  3. Hold both and both lights come on.
  4. Let go of everything and both go out.
💡 Clue 1 — I don’t know where to start

Four things, four names. This is the same lesson as the three LEDs in Activity 1 and the two buttons in Activity 3: one nickname can only ever point at one pin.

  • SW1 is 2, SW2 is 3
  • LED1 red is 11, LED3 green is 13

💡 Clue 2 — do I need a cleverer question?

No. Resist every urge to be clever here. You do not need a way of asking about two buttons in one question — that is the whole of Activity 5, and it is not needed for this.

What you need is the block you have already written, twice, one after the other, both inside the same loop. The second one is a copy of the first with two names changed.

Ask yourself what the Pico does each time round the loop: it takes one of two roads for the red light, finishes that, and then arrives at a completely fresh fork for the green one.

Four names at the top, two decisions in the loop, one wait. The two blocks are complete strangers: nothing in the second one mentions SW1 or the red light, and nothing in the first mentions SW2 or the green one. That is exactly why all four combinations work without you having to think about them.

In Wokwi you will need a second pushbutton on GP3 and a second LED — green, with its own 330 Ω resistor — on GP13. Same wiring rules as the first pair.

📖 Words to remember

ifDo the indented lines underneath, but only when the question is true.
elseAnd do these other lines when it is not. Never on its own, never with a question of its own.
ConditionThe question an if asks. It can only ever come back true or false.
BlockThe lines that belong to an if or an else, marked out by being pushed in.
=One equals: puts something into a name. An order.
==Two equals: asks whether two things are the same. A question.
SyntaxErrorPython cannot read the line at all. Nothing runs. Usually a missing equals sign, colon or bracket.
IndentationErrorA line is at a distance from the left edge that Python was not expecting.
