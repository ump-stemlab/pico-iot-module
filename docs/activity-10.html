🎯 Your mission

Make the board measure the weather in this room and send that measurement out over WiFi, so it draws itself as a live graph on the internet.

Nobody has to be near the board to see it. That is the entire point, and it is the thing that turns a gadget on a desk into what people mean when they say Internet of Things.

What you will learn

Joining a network

Three lines that do what your phone does when it hops onto the WiFi — and one of them waits.

A middleman called MQTT

Small devices do not talk to graphs. They hand their message to a server whose whole job is passing messages on.

A third part on the same bus

A weather sensor joins GP0 and GP1 alongside the screen and the tilt sensor. Still no new pins.

Reshaping a reading, again

This one arrives with its unit stuck on the end. A graph cannot draw C, so the letter has to come off first.

This one needs a Pico W The W is the version with a radio in it. A plain Pico has no WiFi at all and cannot do today’s activity on any network. The LilEx5 has a Pico W on it, and in Wokwi you have to pick the Pico W too — the starter project below already has.
You need Activities 1 to 8 first The forever-loop and how to stop it, print(), variables and comments, not, the I²C bus, adding a library, and the habit of reshaping a reading before you use it. Today leans on every one of them and re-teaches none.

🌡️ A third part on the bus you already built

Today’s reading comes from a weather sensor — temperature, air pressure and humidity, all from one small part. It needs no new pins, because you built the road it travels on two activities ago.

0x76 is already the right form — do not halve it Activity 7 warned you that a datasheet writes the screen’s address as 0x78 while MicroPython wants 0x3C. That is true of the screen and only the screen. This sensor’s 0x76 is already the number MicroPython wants. In any case you never type it: the library goes and finds the sensor for itself.

📶 Joining a network — the first half of the new idea

Before anything can leave the room, the Pico has to get onto the WiFi — the same job your phone does silently every time you walk through a door.

It takes three instructions and then a wait. The wait is the interesting part.

The line that waits

You already know while from Activity 2, where it ran for ever. And you already know not from Activity 6, where it turned an answer round. Today they work together for the first time — and the result is a loop that stops on its own.

Code is shown as a picture.

Read it out loud: while it is not connected, keep going round. The moment the joining finishes, the answer changes, not turns it into a no, and the loop lets go. It is the first loop in this whole module that ends by itself.

2.4 GHz only, and no login pages A Pico W joins 2.4 GHz networks and not 5 GHz ones — if your home router shows two names, the Pico needs the other one. And it cannot get past a network that makes you tick a box on a web page first, because it has no browser to tick it in. Many school networks work exactly like that; a phone hotspot is the usual way round it.

📩 The middleman — the second half of the new idea

Being on the WiFi is not the same as having somewhere to send things. A small board does not talk to a graph directly. It hands its message to a server whose only job is passing messages on, and walks away.

That server is called a broker, and the way of talking to it is called MQTT. It was invented for exactly this: tiny devices, tiny messages, unreliable connections, and no idea who is listening.

Every message needs an address

The broker is handling messages from thousands of boards at once, so every message has to say which box it belongs in. That address is called a topic, and yours is built out of two things you already know: your username and the name of the box.

Two words for the same box The website calls it a feed. MQTT calls the address of it a topic. They are the same thing seen from two sides — the box, and the way you write to it.

🌐 Somewhere for the number to land

A message with nowhere to go is not much of a message. Before you write a line of code, make yourself a place on the internet for the numbers to arrive.

We use Adafruit IO — a free service built for exactly this, run by an electronics company, with no adverts and nothing to install.

1

Make a free account

Go to io.adafruit.com and sign up. It takes about two minutes and costs nothing.

2

Find your username and your key

Click My Key in the top bar. Two things are shown: your username and your active key. Write both down somewhere only you can see.

Your key is a password It lets anybody who has it write to your account as if they were you. Never put it in a screenshot, never paste it into a group chat, and never type it into somebody else’s program. If it does get out, the same page has a button to throw it away and make a new one.
3

Make a feed called temperature

Feeds in the top bar, then + New Feed. Name it temperature, all in lower case — the name becomes part of the address, and the address is fussy about capitals.

Open the feed and click Feed Info. The MQTT address shown there is the line you will put in your program. Copy it exactly.

4

Make a dashboard with a graph on it

Dashboards → + New Dashboard, and name it anything.

Open it, click + New Block, and pick the line chart out of the gallery.

Tick your temperature feed to connect it — a block with no feed attached will sit there for ever showing nothing.

Give the block a title, decide how much history it should show, and create it.

You now have an empty graph waiting for its first point. Leave the tab open — watching it fill up is the best moment in this activity.

The pictures are real, and websites move Every screenshot on this page was taken from Adafruit IO itself. Sites get redesigned, so a button may have shifted since — the words on it will still be the ones written here, so go by those rather than by where it sits.
Who can see your numbers? A feed is private to your account unless you deliberately make it public, so this is not a noticeboard the whole world is reading. It is still the internet, though, and it is still a free account: put the temperature of a room on it, not anything about a person.
Thirty messages a minute, and no more A free account accepts 30 data points per minute. That is why the program waits five seconds between messages — twelve a minute, comfortably inside the limit. Take the wait out and you will be shut out for a while, which looks exactly like a broken program.

2

The broker library, the new way

This one is published properly, under a name, so Thonny can fetch it for you:

Tools → Manage packages…, type micropython-umqtt.simple, press search, then Install.

Thonny puts it on the Pico inside a folder called lib. You will never open it, and that is rather the point — this is what installing a library normally looks like.

Manage packages talks to the Pico, not to your laptop Thonny has to be connected to the board, with nothing running, before the install will work. If the list comes back empty, press stop and try again.
The error that means one is missing ImportError: no module named 'bme280' or ImportError: no module named 'umqtt' both mean the same thing they meant in Activity 7: Python looked on the Pico’s disk and did not find it. Not your laptop’s disk. Two different disks, as ever.

Everything above the loop happens once Joining the network and connecting to the broker are setting up, and they sit outside the loop on purpose. Put them inside and the board rejoins the WiFi every five seconds for ever, which is slow, rude, and gets you thrown off.
🔍 Why is my name for the board in there at all?

The broker keeps one connection per name. If two boards claim the same name, the broker assumes the second one is the first one reconnecting and hangs up on the first. On your own account with one board it makes no difference — but if a whole class shares one account, everybody needs a different name or you will knock each other off all lesson.

🔍 What about the pressure and the humidity?

They arrive at the same instant, in the same answer, and they are the second and the third of the three. Each carries a different unit on its end, and the units are not all one character long — which is the first thing to think about if you go after them in the exercise.

💡 Clue 1 — I don’t know where to start

Do not start a new file. Start from the program you have just written, which already reads the sensor and already publishes, and give it one more thing to do with the reading it already has.

The screen needs two things above the loop, both of them lines you wrote in Activity 7 and have not needed since: its library brought in at the top, and the screen itself made just after the bus. The bus line does not change — the screen is on the bus you have already set up.

Nothing else above the loop changes at all.

💡 Clue 3 — nearly there

Six things to check before you decide it is broken:

  • Is the screen’s library brought in at the top, with the other three?
  • Is the screen made after the bus line and before the loop?
  • Is the wipe inside the loop, and is the show the last thing before the wait? Activity 7’s order, unchanged.
  • Are you taking the reading once at the top and using that one reading for both jobs? Taking it twice is not wrong, but the two will disagree and it will look like a bug.
  • Is the down-number for the second row 16, and is the label still at 0?
  • Is the graph still getting the stripped version, and the screen the full one? They want different things and that is the point.

Test it by warming the sensor and watching both at once. If the screen changes and the graph does not, the publishing is what broke, not the screen.

🌟 Going further

Finished early? Try these — no answers given.

  1. Make a second feed and publish the humidity as well. Its unit is one character long, like the temperature’s, so almost nothing changes.
  2. Now do the pressure. Its unit is not one character long, and finding out how to deal with that is the whole exercise.
  3. Shorten what goes on the screen so it shows one digit after the point instead of two. Activity 8 has the two tools you need, and the order they go in matters.
  4. Publish only when the temperature has moved by more than half a degree since the last message. This needs the board to remember the last one, and it needs a comparison that is not the double equals — a measurement is almost never exactly anything.
  5. Put a Gauge block on your dashboard instead of a line chart, and set its minimum and maximum sensibly for a classroom.
  6. Add a Rule on Adafruit IO that emails you if the temperature goes above a number you choose. You will have to decide what number is worth being woken up for.
  7. Share your dashboard link with somebody in a different room and get them to tell you when you have breathed on the sensor.

📖 Words to remember

WiFiHow the Pico talks to a router without a wire. A Pico W only joins 2.4 GHz networks.
Network nameWhat the network calls itself — the name you pick from the list on your phone.
AddressThe number a network gives your board so messages can find their way back to it.
MQTTA way for small devices to send short messages to a server. Built for exactly this.
BrokerThe server in the middle. It takes messages in and hands them on to whoever asked for them.
PublishSend one message to one address. The board does it and forgets it.
FeedA named box on the website that holds one stream of numbers.
TopicThe address of that box, built from your username and the box’s name.
KeyThe long secret that proves an account is yours. It is a password. Treat it like one.
DashboardA page of blocks — graphs, gauges, switches — showing what is in your feeds.
Rate limitThe most messages a minute an account is allowed. Thirty, on a free one.
Captive portalA network that makes you agree to something on a web page first. A Pico cannot get past one.
