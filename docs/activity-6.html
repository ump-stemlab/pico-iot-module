🎯 Your mission

A two-key safety switch. The red light comes on only while both SW1 and SW2 are held down. One key on its own does nothing at all.

This is a real thing, not a puzzle. Machines that could take a hand off — presses, guillotines, the big red cutters in a workshop — are started with two buttons far enough apart that you need both hands to do it. Two hands on the buttons means no hands anywhere dangerous.

In Activity 5 you wrote two decisions that knew nothing about each other: one button, one light, twice over. Today there is one decision, and its question has two halves.

What you will learn

The word and

Both halves have to be true. The strictest of the three.

The word or

One half is enough — and both is fine too.

The word not

Turns an answer round: yes becomes no, no becomes yes.

Two whole questions

Each side of and has to stand on its own, complete.

You need Activities 1 to 5 first Naming pins, the forever-loop, stopping it, print(), if, else, the double equals and both levels of indentation are all assumed here, and none of them is taught again. If the double equals is still shaky, go back to Activity 5 before you start.

🧭 The new idea — joining two questions

A question can only ever answer yes or no. So can a pair of questions joined together — and that is the whole trick.

You already know how to ask one question. Is SW1 held down? Yes or no. You can ask a second one just as easily. Is SW2 held down? Yes or no.

What you could not do until today is join them, so that the two answers become one answer that an if can use. That is what the joining words are for.

There are three of these words in Python, and they are the three words this activity is about:

WordWhat it doesHow strict it is
andJoins two questions. The answer is yes only when both are yes.The strictest
orJoins two questions. The answer is yes when at least one is yes.The most generous
notSits in front of one question and turns its answer round.Neither — it flips
English is not Python, and this is where people come unstuck In the corridor, “press SW1 and SW2” is a perfectly good sentence. In a program it is not a question at all, and Python will either refuse it or quietly answer something you did not ask. The section on two whole questions is the one to read twice.

↻ The word not, and why it is the fiddliest

Learn it, use it sparingly, and be very careful what you point it at.

not goes in front of one question and turns its answer round. Written out in full, with the question it flips wrapped in brackets so you can see exactly what is being flipped:

Code is shown as a picture — turn on JavaScript to see it.

Read out loud, that is “it is not the case that SW1 is pressed” — in other words, SW1 is not being pressed. The brackets are not compulsory, but put them in anyway. They show you and everybody else where the question being flipped starts and ends.

Do not reach for not to “fix” the button numbers A button reads 0 while it is pressed. That is not a mistake and it does not need undoing — it is how a pull-up works, and it was the whole of Activity 4’s trap. A student who writes not in front of a comparison in order to make it “the right way round” ends up with two negatives stacked on top of each other and no idea which way the answer points. Compare with 0 and leave it alone — see Activity 5.

That asks whether neither button is being pressed — so the light is on when the machine is untouched, and goes out the moment somebody grabs the keys. For a safety switch, that is about as wrong as it is possible to be.

So where is not useful? When the thing you want really is the absence of something: a light that is on until a door closes, a warning that stops when a switch is armed, or the going-further task at the bottom of this page, where a third key must not be held. Reach for it then — and not before.

This is Activity 5’s circuit with one part added: a second pushbutton on GP3. If your Activity 5 project is still open, start from that one — you only have to add a button and two wires.

Draw the six wires

The button rule from Activity 4 has not changed: each one straddles the gap, with one wire from a strip above it and one from a strip below, and no resistor on a button.

In words: three parts, four pads. The two green wires are the ones your program reads and the red one is the light. Every ground now drops to the − rail, so instead of three black wires back to the Pico there is one.

Physical pinName on the boardWhat goes there

Two sets of numbers, and only one of them goes in your code The big white numbers are the physical pins — where a wire physically goes. The names beside them are what you type. GP2 is physical pin 4 and GP3 is physical pin 5; typing 4 or 5 in your program would give you two completely different pins.
Three grounds, one wire Both buttons and the LED drop to the − rail, and the rail is one long piece of metal, so a single wire takes all three back to the Pico. Any of the eight GND pads would have done — pin 18 is simply the closest.

Nothing to wire. SW1 is already joined to GP2, SW2 to GP3 and LED1 to GP11, inside the board, in copper.

🤔 Could I not just write two ifs like last time?

Not for this. Two separate decisions can each switch the light on, but neither one knows what the other found, so nothing can insist on both at once. You would end up switching the light on in one block and off again in the next, and the result depends on which block ran last.

That is exactly the situation and exists for: one question, one answer, one decision. When a thing depends on two facts together, join the facts — do not split the decision.

🚀 Exercise — the two-of-three keypad

A lock that opens for any two of three keys. It needs and and or in the same question — and a little thinking before you type anything.

Task: three buttons, one light. The green LED comes on when at least two of SW1, SW2 and SW3 are held down at the same time. It does not matter which two.

  • SW1 is 2, SW2 is 3, SW3 is 4
  • LED3, the green one, is 13
💡 Clue 1 — I don’t know where to start

Do not write any code yet. On paper, list every way of holding exactly two of the three keys. There are only a few, and once they are written down the rest is typing.

Then ask yourself: to open the lock, do you need all of the ways on your list to be happening at once, or just one of them? The answer to that question tells you which joining word goes between them.

💡 Clue 2 — how do I write “one pair”?

You already have. One pair of keys held together is exactly the question you wrote for the two-key safety switch:

Code is shown as a picture — turn on JavaScript to see it.

Your list from Clue 1 has more than one pair on it. Write each pair the same way, and then join the pairs together with the other joining word — because any one of them opening the lock is enough.

Put brackets around each pair. They are not compulsory, but with three pairs in one line they are the difference between a line you can read and a line you cannot.

💡 Clue 3 — nearly there

The finished question is one line, and it is a long one. That is normal and it is not a mistake — the editor will let it run off the right-hand side, and Python does not mind.

Three things to check before you run it:

  • Every single half is a whole question: a value, two equals signs, and a 0. Six of them altogether.
  • The word inside each pair of brackets is not the same as the word between them.
  • The rest of the program — the loop, the decision, the two roads and the wait — is identical to the one you have already written. Only the question changed.

Test it by holding SW1 and SW3 — the pair people most often leave off the list.

No answer on this page There is no answer block here on purpose — work it out from the clues, and check it by pressing the keys rather than by reading. Your teacher has the worked answer if the class needs it.

🌟 Going further

Finished early? Try these — no answers given.

  1. Make the keypad accept exactly two keys and refuse all three. This is where not earns its place.
  2. Build a door alarm: the slide switch SW4 on 15 arms it, and the buzzer BZ1 on 14 sounds only when it is armed and SW1 is pressed. Find out for yourself which way round SW4 reads — print it and look.
  3. Add the green LED to the two-key switch as a “safe” light: green on whenever the red one is off, and out when the machine is running.
  4. Make a doorbell out of or: either SW1 or SW2 sounds the buzzer.
  5. Three keys, all three needed. How many joining words does that question have in it?
  6. Take the two-key switch and make it need SW1 held down and SW2 not held. Predict what it will do before you run it.

📖 Words to remember

andJoins two whole questions. Yes only when both halves are yes.
orJoins two whole questions. Yes when at least one half is yes — both included.
notGoes in front of one question and turns its answer round.
ConditionThe question an if asks. It can now have several halves, and it still gives exactly one answer.
BooleanThe proper name for a value that can only be yes or no — true or false. Everything on this page is boolean logic.
BracketsPut round a half to show where it starts and ends. Rarely compulsory, always kind to the next person reading it.
