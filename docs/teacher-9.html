Activity 9 — teacher notes

Soil Moisture. The first analogue pin in the module — and the first time a sensor’s numbers mean nothing until the class has gone and found out what they mean.

▶ Student page 📚 All teacher notes ← Activity 8 notes 📌 Pin reference
Keep this link to yourself. Unlisted and hidden from search engines, but the repository is public — anyone with the URL can read it.

What is being taught

  • The one idea is the analogue pin. Everything before today was on or off. A pin that answers with a position on a scale is the new thing, and the ADC is how it does it.
  • Calibration is the lesson they will remember, and it is on the student page on purpose, not just here. A raw reading means nothing until somebody has measured both ends. Do not let it get cut for time.
  • Calibration comes after the program runs, deliberately. They run with 50000 and 25000, find them wrong for their own cup, and fix them. Slower than handing out the numbers, and far more memorable. This was Kamil’s call.
  • Big number means dry. Backwards from everyone’s expectation. Wet soil conducts, so the voltage falls. Say it before the first reading appears, not after.
  • elif arrives here, and only here. Kept out of the whole module up to now on purpose; three zones is the first honest reason for it. Teach it as the second new idea and do not apologise for it. The module-wide “no elif” rule ends today.
  • // is new too, and small: whole-number division, because a percentage has no use for a tail of digits.
  • First activity that wires anything to the real board. Everything before this was already soldered on. Budget time for three jumper wires and for the power-off rule.
  • Reused, not re-taught: the loop, sleep, print(), if/else, variables, comments, indentation, and > plus the idea of a threshold, which are Activity 8’s. < appears for the first time in the clamp lines and is one sentence of work.
  • No for loops, still.

The pin, and the three numbers — check this before you teach it

  • The probe goes on the 40-pin header H1, not on the H3 analogue connector. H3’s four analogue inputs go through a separate converter chip and come back over I²C; nothing in this program can read them. A group sent to H3 will look like they have a dead sensor.
  • Signal → header pin 18, top row, printed GPIO24, which is the Pico’s GP26. Three numbers, one hole. The student page gives them one column each and never mixes two in a sentence.
  • Power → header pin 1, bottom row, far left. It is the only 3V3 on the whole header. Pin 17 is not connected; pins 2 and 4 are 5 V and sit in the same corner as pin 1.
  • Ground → header pin 20, top row, immediately beside pin 18. Any GND would do; this one keeps the wiring tidy.
  • GP27 (printed GPIO23, header pin 16) is the free second analogue pin, for the two-probe going-further task. GP28 (printed GPIO18, header pin 12) is spoken for by the servo activity.

The Wokwi route — what was actually verified

  • Starter project: wokwi.com/projects/472418654786566145 — a plain wokwi-pi-pico (not a Pico W) plus the STEM Lab’s custom chip-soil-moisture, wired gold to GP26, red to 3V3, black to a ground.
  • main.py is empty on purpose. If a group opens it and finds code, they are in somebody’s fork.
  • The slider is not discoverable. Press play, then click the green block. One control, Water in the soil (%), starting at 15. Demonstrate it on the projector.
  • Measured in the running simulator during this build: water 0 % → 65535, water 15 % → 55693, water 61 % → 25558, water 100 % → 0. Dead linear across the full range.
  • The printed 50000 / 25000 work in Wokwi and sit at about 24 % and 62 % water, so the three zones are all reachable with the slider.
  • Calibrating in Wokwi gives 65535 and 0, because the simulated sensor uses the whole scale. Put those in and the printed percentage becomes the slider position exactly — checked for all 101 positions. That is a free correctness check for the whole sum and it is on the student page.
  • The custom chip cannot corrode, drift or be pushed in too deep, which is worth saying out loud: Route A teaches the arithmetic honestly and the physical care not at all.
  • If a later builder changes the program, run it again. The cloud container cannot reach wokwi.com; this needs a browser.

Exercise answer 
# Activity 9 exercise - a light instead of a word
from machine import ADC, Pin
from time import sleep

soil = ADC(Pin(26))
red = Pin(11, Pin.OUT)         # LED1
green = Pin(13, Pin.OUT)       # LED3

dry = 50000
wet = 25000

while True:
    reading = soil.read_u16()
    print(reading)

    percent = (dry - reading) * 100 // (dry - wet)
    if percent < 0:
        percent = 0
    if percent > 100:
        percent = 100
    print(percent)

    if reading > dry:
        print("DRY")
        red.value(1)               # thirsty
        green.value(0)
    elif reading > wet:
        print("JUST RIGHT")
        red.value(0)               # neither light
        green.value(0)
    else:
        print("WET")
        red.value(0)
        green.value(1)             # plenty

    sleep(1)
  • Every branch deals with both lights. That is the whole exercise. A branch that only turns its own light on leaves the other one stuck from the previous round.
  • The middle branch is the one they leave empty. It is also the one that makes the thing work. Expect to point at it.
  • Same pins on both routes — GP11 and GP13. Deliberate: the deck used different pins in the simulator, which breaks the page’s promise that the program is identical. On the real LilEx5 there is nothing to wire.
  • Accept the decision written against percent instead of reading — it is the same decision, and it is going-further task 4. It does need the comparisons turning round, which is a good thing to make them explain.
  • Accept the lights set before the printing, or after. Do not make a thing of the order.
  • The most instructive wrong answer is two separate if blocks instead of the existing three-way decision. It works, it is longer, and asking why the first version is better is the whole point of elif.
If a group has already calibrated Their dry and wet will not be 50000 and 25000, and that is correct. The answer above keeps the printed pair only so it reads against the student page.

Before the lesson

  • Test every probe. Plug each one in and run the calibration program on it. A corroded probe looks exactly like a wiring mistake and will eat a whole group’s lesson.
  • Prepare cups of soil at three wetnesses — bone dry, damp, and freshly watered — so a group that gets a working reading has somewhere to go next.
  • One probe, three female-to-female jumpers and a cup per pair. Paper and a pen too: they write two numbers down and the numbers matter for the rest of the lesson.
  • Do the calibration yourself first, on one of these probes in this soil, and keep the two numbers. You want to know roughly what the class should be getting.
  • Water in something with a small spout. “A little at a time” is the whole experiment, and a full jug ends it in one go.
  • Paper towel on the desks, and somewhere to put a wet probe down.
  • Wokwi reachable from the classroom, and the starter project link on the board.
  • Check the pin reference is bookmarked — tapping a pin on it is faster and safer than counting to eighteen along a black header.
Looking after the probes afterwards Rinse, dry, and do not leave them standing in wet soil between lessons. A resistive probe corrodes while it is powered and wet, and a set left in cups over a weekend will be visibly worse on Monday. Store them dry, with their calibration numbers on a sticky note.

Teaching notes

  • Open on the contrast with Activity 8. That sensor also answered how much, but over a bus, with a library doing the work. This one is a voltage on a wire and nothing in between. That is what makes it worth its own lesson.
  • Draw the two signal graphs on the board before they open the page. Square wave, then a wandering line. Ask which one soil looks like. It takes ninety seconds and it does most of the teaching.
  • Answer “why does more water give a smaller number?” before it is asked. Wet soil conducts, so the voltage drops. If you wait, half the class will have decided their probe is broken.
  • Walk the percentage line through with real numbers on the board before anybody types it. It is the only arithmetic in the module and it is one line with two brackets in it.
  • Let somebody hit 138 % before you explain the two clamp lines. It is a much better hook than a rule.
  • Do not hand out calibration numbers. The whole point is that they cannot be handed out. If a group is stuck, ask them what the probe reads in the air, not what number to type.
  • Expect the groups to disagree with each other, and say in advance that they will. Two probes in two cups genuinely have different numbers; neither group is wrong.
  • The awkward question is on the page on purpose. What if the two numbers are the wrong way round? Let them try it rather than telling them. Nothing breaks, and every answer is wrong, which is the most useful ten seconds of the lesson.
  • On elif: teach it as three parts of one decision, at one indent, asked in order and stopping at the first yes. Do not introduce chains longer than three; three zones is the reason it is here.
  • If you are short of time, cut the exercise, not the calibration. The exercise is Activity 1 and Activity 5 combined and they can do it next lesson; the calibration is the only place in the module where this idea appears.
  • Timing, roughly: 10 minutes on analogue and the ADC, 10 on wiring, 15 on the program, 10 on calibrating, 15 on the exercise.
