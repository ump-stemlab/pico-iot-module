🎯 Your mission

Put your own words on the little screen — and leave them there. Two lines of text, written by your program, glowing on the board itself.

It sounds small. It is not. Every light and every buzz so far has been the board saying something happened. A screen is the board saying what happened, in words, with nobody’s laptop attached. Unplug the USB cable, put the board on a battery, and the words are still there.

What you will learn

A screen of its own

128 by 64 tiny lights. Words go on it in rows, at positions you choose.

A bus, not a wire

Two wires that eight different parts share, each with its own address.

Your first library

A file of ready-made instructions that has to be put on the Pico before it will work.

Write, then show

Words go onto a page in memory first. Nothing appears until you say show.

You need Activities 1 to 6 first Naming a pin, the forever-loop and how to stop it, print(), if and else, the double equals, and / or / not, comments and both levels of indentation are all assumed here, and none of them is taught again.

🖥️ Meet the screen

Your board has a small OLED screen above the buttons. OLED means every dot on it makes its own light — there is no backlight behind it, which is why the black parts are properly black and why it is readable from the side.

It is a grid of tiny lights, and nothing else

The screen is 128 dots across and 64 dots down. Each dot — each pixel — is either on or off. There is no grey and no colour. Everything you will ever put on this screen is made of those 8192 dots.

Down is measured downwards In maths lessons, bigger numbers go up. On a screen they go down: 0 is the top row and 63 is the bottom one. Every screen ever made counts this way, so it is worth getting used to now rather than arguing with it later.

A letter is eight pixels wide and eight tall

You will not be switching pixels on one at a time. The library does that for you: you hand it some words and a position, and it draws the letters. Each letter is a block 8 pixels wide and 8 pixels tall, which is the only size this screen’s built-in lettering comes in.

That gives the screen exactly 16 letters across and 8 rows down. If you count on your fingers you will get the same numbers: 128 ÷ 8 is 16, and 64 ÷ 8 is 8.

Which pins it uses

WireWhat it carriesPin you name in code
SDAThe data — the words themselves, one bit at a time0
SCLThe clock — a tick that says “here comes the next bit”1
VCCPower in, 3.3 V— not a GP pin
GNDGround, the return path— not a GP pin
Two wires do the talking, two wires do the feeding Only SDA and SCL carry anything you will ever type. VCC and GND are there because a screen needs electricity, exactly like the LED did — no program mentions them.

🚒 Two wires, eight parts — what a bus is

Every part you have used so far had a pin to itself. The screen does not, and neither will any of the sensors. They all share one pair of wires called a bus.

Think about what would happen if each part had its own wires. The LilEx5 carries a screen, a temperature sensor, a motion sensor, a compass, a light sensor, an air sensor and more — eight parts in all. Two wires each is sixteen pins, and the Pico has twenty-six.

The shared pair is called I²C — say it “eye-squared-see”. One wire, SDA, carries the message. The other, SCL, ticks like a metronome so that both ends agree where one bit ends and the next begins.

If they all share, how does one part know it is being spoken to?

Every part on the bus has a number of its own, called its address. The Pico puts the address on the wire first, then the message. Seven parts hear their own address is not being called and ignore everything that follows; one part hears its own and listens.

Why the addresses look so strange 0x3C is a number written in hexadecimal — a way of writing numbers that hardware people prefer. In the ordinary numbers you count with, 0x3C is 60. You do not need to be able to convert them; you need to be able to recognise one when a datasheet hands you one.
The same screen, two different numbers — and both are “right” A datasheet for this screen will very often print its address as 0x78. That is the same address, written in the style that includes an extra bit on the end for “am I speaking or listening”. MicroPython wants the plain form, 0x3C. You are not going to type either of them today — the library already knows — but when you meet 0x78 in a datasheet later, it is not a different screen.

📚 Adding a library — the second new idea

Python has come with everything you needed so far. It does not come with this. The screen and the library are one lesson, not two: the screen is the reason to add a library, and the library is the only way to use the screen.

Type that today and Python will say it has never heard of it, because ssd1306 is a file that somebody wrote and you have to put it on the Pico yourself. That file is a library: a few hundred lines that turn the tedious business of talking to a screen chip into three or four instructions you can actually use.

SSD1306 is the name of the chip, not the name of the screen Behind the glass is a little controller chip called an SSD1306, and it is the chip the library knows how to drive. Thousands of different screens use it, which is exactly why one library file is worth writing and sharing.

Where the file has to go

The Pico has a small disk inside it. Your program lives on it, and the library file has to live on it too, beside your program. If it is on your laptop, in your downloads, or open in an editor window, that is not the same thing — it has to be on the Pico.

Getting it there — and the two routes are genuinely different

This is the one place in the whole module where Wokwi and the real board do not do the same thing. Read the one that applies to you, and skim the other so you know what your neighbour is doing.

1

Open the file menu

Above the code editor there are two tabs, main.py and diagram.json, and a small ▾ arrow beside them. Click the arrow. The menu has Rename, Delete, New file… and Upload file(s)….

2

Make a new file called ssd1306.py

Choose New file… and type the name exactly: lower case, and ending in .py. A third tab appears, empty.

3

Paste the library into it

Open the library file from the STEM LAB library repository, select all of it, and paste it into the new tab.

Yes, paste. This once. Everywhere else on this site you type things out, because typing is how you learn them. A library is different: it is somebody else’s tool, you are never going to read all of it, and nobody — not even engineers — retypes one. Copy it. Then go back to typing your own program.
4

Go back to main.py

Your own program still goes there. Wokwi puts every file in the project onto the simulated Pico when you press ▶ Play, so the import line will now find it.

1

Get the file onto your computer

Download ssd1306.py from the STEM LAB library repository. Your teacher may already have it on a shared drive.

2

Open it in Thonny

File → Open…, choose This computer, and open ssd1306.py. Do not change anything in it.

3

Save it onto the Pico

File → Save as… and — this is the step everybody misses — choose Raspberry Pi Pico rather than This computer. Keep the name ssd1306.py exactly.

Saving it on your computer again does nothing The file is already on your computer. If the dialogue did not ask you where, the Pico is not connected — check the bottom-right corner of Thonny first.
4

Check it landed

Turn on View → Files. The lower panel lists what is on the Pico. You should see ssd1306.py there. It stays there for good — you will not have to do this again in Activity 8.

📦 The other way, if your computer has internet in the lab

Tools → Manage packages…, search for micropython-ssd1306, and press Install. Thonny fetches it and puts it in a lib folder on the Pico for you. It is the same file arriving by a different road, and the import line does not change.

The error message you will meet, and what it really means ImportError: no module named 'ssd1306' does not mean your program is wrong. It means Python looked on the Pico’s disk for a file with that name and did not find one. Nine times out of ten the file is on the computer instead of the Pico, or it is spelt SSD1306.py or ssd1306.py.txt. Fix the file, not the program.

🔧 Build the circuit

Start a fresh project this time. There is nothing from Activity 6 worth keeping — no buttons, no LED, no resistor. A Pico, a screen and four wires.

1

Add the screen

Click the blue + and type ssd1306. One part comes back: SSD1306 OLED display, under Display. Add it and drag it clear of the Pico so you can see all four of its pads.

2

Draw the four wires

The screen has four pads in a row along its top edge, labelled in tiny white letters: GND, VCC, SCL, SDA. There is no resistor and nothing is diagonal — each pad goes to exactly one pin.

In words: the green and blue wires are the two your program uses. The black one is ground and the red one is power — they are long because the 3.3 V pin is on the other side of the board, and that is fine. A wire’s length means nothing.

Physical pinName on the boardScreen pad
1GP0SDA — the data
2GP1SCL — the clock
3GNDGND
363V3VCC — power in
Two sets of numbers again, and it matters more than usual here GP0 is physical pin 1 and GP1 is physical pin 2. For once the two numbers are almost the same, which makes it easier to mix them up, not harder. The wire goes to the pin; the number you type is the GP.
VCC goes to 3V3, not to 5V Physical pin 36 is the 3.3 V supply and it is the one to use. Pins 39 and 40 also look like power and are not — they are 5 V, and 5 V is the one rule this module has repeated since Activity 1.
Any GND pad still works Physical pin 3 is drawn here only because it is nearest. All eight grounds are the same piece of metal inside the Pico.

Nothing to wire. The screen is already joined to GP0 and GP1 inside the board, in copper, along with every sensor on it.

1

Plug in and power up

USB cable in, power switch SW5 on.

2

Find the Pico in Thonny

Bottom-right corner of Thonny, choose MicroPython (Raspberry Pi Pico), and wait for >>> in the Shell.

3

Make sure the library is on the Pico

This is the step that replaces wiring for you. Go back to Adding a library and finish it before you write a line of the program.

The safety rules have not gone away Never connect a 5V pin to a GP pin — GP pins are 3.3 V only. Never join a power pin to a GND pin. Switch the board off at SW5 before changing any wires.

Write, then show — and why it works that way

The single most common thing that goes wrong today A program with no show at the end behaves exactly like a program that ran perfectly. No error, no warning, no clue. If your screen is black, look at the last line first.

💡 Clue 3 — nearly there

Five things to check before you decide it is broken:

  • Is the wipe inside the loop? Outside it, the page is only cleaned once and everything piles up.
  • Is the show the last thing before the wait? Anything written after it will not appear until the next round.
  • Are you comparing with 0? A button reads 0 while it is pressed — Activity 4’s trap, and it has not changed.
  • Are the two rows far enough apart? 0 and 16.
  • Do the two buttons have their own names and their own decisions? Two labels, two words, two if/else pairs, both at four spaces.

Test it by holding SW2 on its own. If the first line changes too, the second decision is reading the first button.

🌟 Going further

Finished early? Try these — no answers given.

  1. Move a word to the middle of the screen. You will have to work out the across-number yourself: count the letters, multiply by 8, and take that away from 128.
  2. Make the screen show BOTH on a third row when SW1 and SW2 are held together — one more decision, using and from Activity 6.
  3. Light LED1 on 11 at the same moment the screen says HELD, so the light and the words agree. If they ever disagree, you have found a real bug.
  4. Fill the page with 1 instead of 0 and see what happens to your words. Explain it before you undo it.
  5. Put the slide switch SW4 on 15 on a fourth row. Find out for yourself which way round it reads.
  6. Count how many rows you can fit before words start falling off the bottom. Predict the number first.
