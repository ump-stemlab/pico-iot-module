Servo Motor

Until now every output was a light. Today you move something. A servo motor has an arm that swings to a precise angle on command — no guessing, no drifting. You tell it exactly where to point, and it goes there.

🤖 What is a servo motor?

A servo is a motor with a brain attached. The brain watches a pulse signal and keeps the arm pointed wherever that pulse says — even if something pushes back.

Inside the SG90 there are three things: a small DC motor, a gear train that makes the movement precise, and a tiny circuit that listens for pulses. You send a short pulse, it measures it, turns the motor until its arm matches, and stops. It holds that position until you send a different pulse. That is all a servo does — but it is enough to open a door, aim a camera, steer a robot, or move any mechanical part to a known angle.

The arm does not spin all the way round like a wheel. It swings through about 180° — from left to right — and the pulse length decides where it stops inside that arc.

One servo per pair. On the real board, you will share one servo between two people. In Wokwi each person simulates their own. The program is identical either way.

🔌 How the Pico moves it (PWM)

PWM stands for Pulse Width Modulation. Instead of sending a steady voltage, the pin flickers on and off very quickly. The length of each “on” burst is what the servo reads.

You already used PWM without knowing it: in Activity 2, the LED dimmed because the pin was on for a short fraction of each flicker cycle. The servo uses the same flickering, but it cares about the exact length of one pulse — not about how bright a lamp gets.

🔁 The three numbers

The Pico’s duty scale runs from 0 to 65535. You do not use the whole scale — only a small slice near the bottom.

These numbers correspond to pulse lengths: 1638 is a very short pulse (about 0.5 ms), 4915 is a medium one (1.5 ms), and 8192 is a long one (2.5 ms). The servo motor was built to understand these pulse lengths as positions — no arithmetic needed on your part. You just pass the number and the arm goes there.

Your program stores the three numbers in variables so the names (left, middle, right) appear in the code instead of bare numbers. That makes it easy to read and easy to change later.

⚠️ The pin trap: three names, one hole

The signal wire goes to the hole labelled GPIO18 on the LilEx5 board. But in your program you type GP28. And the connection table says pin 12. Same hole — three names.

Name in your codeName on the LilEx5 silkscreenPhysical header pin
GP28GPIO18H1 pin 12 (top row, 6th pad from the left end)

Why the mismatch? The LilEx5 board remaps the Pico’s pins through its own header to save space. The silkscreen prints the header’s own numbering, not the Pico’s GPIO number. Your code talks to the Pico, so you use GP28. Your wire goes to the header hole, so you count along the top row to pin 12.

You will meet this same situation in every activity that uses the header. The Pin reference page has the full translation table.

Two boards, two sets of numbers. In Wokwi you wire a bare Raspberry Pi Pico, so the numbers are the Pico’s own physical pins. On the LilEx5 the wires go into H1, the 40-pin header along the top edge, and the numbers are H1’s. The line you type — Pin(28) — is the same either way.

Share the servo. One physical servo is shared between two people at the same board. Disconnect the servo before the other person runs their code, then reconnect it for yours. In Wokwi there is no sharing — each person gets their own simulation.

On the real LilEx5 board the wires go into H1 — the black 40-pin header that runs along the top edge. Pin 1 is the left-hand end of the bottom row, the corner nearest the CN2 connector. Even pins run along the top row, nearest the edge of the board, and all three of your wires go into that top row.

The servo connector snaps onto three adjacent pins; these three are not adjacent on H1, so you will use three separate jumper wires, one per servo lead. Match the wire colours carefully — connecting V+ to GND will not break the board, but the servo will not move.

Watch the row. Pin 1 (3V3) sits below pin 2 (+5V), not beside it, so a wire one row out lands on 3.3 V and the servo only twitches.

🎯 Going further

The numbers 1638, 4915, and 8192 are three points on a continuous scale. Any whole number between 1638 and 8192 is a valid position — not just three of them. If you choose a number halfway between left and middle (about 3276) the arm will stop there. Later activities will show how to compute positions mathematically and how to sweep smoothly — but those tools have not been introduced yet, so for now explore by experimenting with numbers.

You can also connect a servo to a different GP pin by changing the number inside Code is shown as a picture.. Any of the GP pins that do not already have a job can drive a servo. The duty values stay the same regardless of which pin you use.

📖 Words to remember

Servo motorA motor with a built-in circuit that moves its arm to a precise angle based on the pulse it receives.
PWMPulse Width Modulation. A pin that flickers on and off rapidly. The length of each “on” burst carries the signal.
Duty valueThe number you pass to duty_u16(). It sets how long each pulse lasts. Range 0–65535; for this servo you use 1638–8192.
VBUSThe 5 V supply drawn straight from the USB cable. Header pin 2 on the LilEx5. The servo needs this — not 3V3.
PWM(Pin(28))Creates a PWM-controlled connection to GP28. You must call this before you can set a duty value.
freq(50)Sets 50 pulses per second, which is the standard all hobby servos expect. Call it once, right after creating the PWM object.
duty_u16()Sets the pulse length. Pass 1638, 4915, or 8192 (or any number in between) to move the arm.
print()Sends a message to the Shell panel. Useful for seeing what your program is doing while it runs.
