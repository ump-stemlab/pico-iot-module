Sensors and Numbers

Every answer the board has given you so far was one you put there yourself. Today it measures something — and what comes back is not a 0 or a 1. It is a number with a decimal point and a long tail of digits, and you have to do something about that before it will fit on a screen.

🎯 Your mission

Make the board measure how it is tilted, and put that measurement on its own screen — rounded to something a human can read.

Pick the board up and turn it, and the number changes. Put it down and it settles. Nothing you typed decided that number: the board found it out for itself, and that is a genuinely different kind of program from every one you have written so far.

What you will learn

A sensor measures

A button is yes or no. A sensor answers with how much — and the answer lands anywhere in between.

No new pins at all

The sensor sits on the very same two wires the screen does. That is what a bus was for.

Numbers with a point in them

Why a measurement has a fractional part, and why seven digits come back when you only wanted one.

round(), words, and >

Shorten the number, turn it into letters, and ask which side of a line it has landed on.

You need Activities 1 to 7 first The forever-loop and how to stop it, print(), if and else, variables and comments, the I²C bus, and the whole business of getting a library file onto the Pico. Today reuses all of it and re-teaches none of it.

🌐 What a sensor actually is

Every input you have read so far could only ever be one of two things. A sensor is the first part on this board that answers with how much.

Press SW1 and it reads 0. Let go and it reads 1. There is nothing in between, and there never can be — a button is shut or it is open. A sensor is not like that. It is measuring something real, and real things do not come in two sizes.

The sensor on this board, and what it measures

The LilEx5 carries an accelerometer. Its job is to notice which way is down — and because gravity always points down, an accelerometer that is sitting still is really a very good tilt meter.

It answers three times over, one number for each direction, and each number is measured in g — the strength of gravity itself. A board lying flat on the desk has all of gravity pulling straight through it, so one of the three numbers is about 1 and the other two are about 0. Stand the board on its edge and the numbers swap over.

Which of the three is which? Find out, do not guess. The three directions are called x, y and z, but which one points which way depends on how the chip happens to be soldered onto the board. In the simulator the sliders are labelled, so it is obvious. On the real board, print all three and tip it about until you have worked out which is which — that is a two-minute experiment and it is worth more than being told.

🔗 A second part on a bus you already built

Here is the payoff for Activity 7. The sensor needs no new pins and no new wires — it goes on the same pair the screen is already using. One more part, one more address, and nothing else changes.

That is exactly what a bus is for, and you have already done all the hard part. Activity 7’s bus section has the whole story if you want it again; the short version is that GP0 carries the data and GP1 carries the clock, every part hears everything, and each part has an address so it knows which messages are meant for it.

The sensor’s address is 0x68, and this one needs no halving Activity 7 warned you that a screen’s datasheet often prints 0x78 for what MicroPython calls 0x3C. That is a real trap, but it is not a rule that applies to every number you meet. This sensor’s address is 0x68 in both styles, which is the form the library already uses. As in Activity 7, no program on this page types an address at all — the library finds the sensor by itself.

🔢 Decimals, and what round() is for — the second new idea

The sensor is the reason decimals turn up. round() is the only thing that makes them fit. They are one lesson, not two — exactly the way the screen and the library were one lesson last time.

Where the decimal point comes from

Every number the board has handed you so far has been a whole one: 0, 1, a pin number, a count. That is because they were all counted. A measurement is not counted, it is compared — and the thing being compared almost never lands exactly on a whole number.

Inside the sensor is a tiny weight on tiny springs, and the chip reports where the weight is sitting as a whole number of very small steps. There are 16 384 steps in one g. So every answer is some whole number of those steps, divided by 16 384 — and a fraction like that almost never comes out as a tidy decimal.

Why seven digits, and not two or twenty? Seven is roughly as much as MicroPython can hold on to for a number this size, so seven is what it prints. The digits are real — they are just far more precision than a person tilting a board could ever use. Nothing is wrong. There is simply too much answer.

What round() does about it

The 1 is how many digits to keep after the point. Everything past that is gone — not hidden, not stored somewhere else, gone — and the last digit kept is nudged up or down to whichever is nearer.

Rounding is a one-way street Once you have rounded a reading you cannot get the rest of it back. That is fine when you are putting it on a screen for a person to look at, and it is not fine if you were going to do sums with it afterwards. Round last, at the moment you show something — never as soon as the reading arrives.

And now the part that catches everybody: a number is not words

You have a short, tidy number. You try to put it on the screen the way Activity 7 taught you, and the program stops dead with a complaint about types.

Here is the reason, and it is worth thirty seconds because it is the only genuinely new piece of syntax in the whole activity. 0.9 and "0.9" are two different things. One is a quantity — you can halve it, add to it, compare it. The other is three letters in a row: a zero, a dot and a nine. The screen draws letters. It has no idea what to do with a quantity.

So the line that puts a reading on the screen does two jobs on the way, reading from the inside out: round the number, then turn the short number into words.

Read it inside out, like a set of boxes The innermost bracket happens first. round gets the reading and hands back a shorter number; str gets that shorter number and hands back the letters; and only then does the screen get anything. If you find that hard to see, put the two steps on two separate lines with a name in between — it does exactly the same thing and there is no shame in it.

⚖️ Asking whether a measurement is big enough

There is one more thing a decimal changes, and it is the reason Activity 5’s question does not work here. == asks is it exactly this? — and a measurement is almost never exactly anything. Watch the Shell for ten seconds: the number never once lands on a whole one, and it never will.

So there is a third comparison, and it is the one every measurement needs: > — more than. It does not ask for a single value; it asks which side of a line the reading has landed on, and a reading always lands on one side or the other.

Code is shown as a picture — turn on JavaScript to see it.
Pick the line yourself, and pick it away from where the reading sits There is no right answer to “how much is tilted”. Half a g is a sensible line because it is a long way from both flat and on-edge, so a reading wobbling by a hundredth will never flip the answer back and forth. A line drawn at 0.99 would flicker all day.
< exists too, and it is the mirror image Less than. Everything on this page about > is true of it the other way round, and the two of them together are how every threshold in every program you will ever write is built.

📚 The sensor’s library — the same job as last time, twice

Nothing new to learn here. This is Activity 7’s library step again, with two files instead of one, and ssd1306.py is already where you left it.

The screen needed ssd1306.py. The sensor needs imu.py — and imu.py itself needs vector3d.py, because whoever wrote it borrowed a tool of their own. That is completely normal and it is the only thing about today’s library step that is different: a library is allowed to need another library. Copy both and it works; copy one and you get the same “no module named” message, about the other name.

🔧 Add the sensor to the circuit

Four more wires, and not one of them goes anywhere new.

Do not start a fresh project. Open the one from Activity 7, with the screen already wired and ssd1306.py already in it, and add to it.

1

Add the sensor part

Click the blue + and type mpu6050. One part comes back: MPU6050 Accelerometer + Gyroscope. Add it and drag it somewhere clear, with its row of pads facing the Pico.

2

Draw the four wires

The sensor has eight pads along its top edge and you are only using four of them. Reading left to right they are INT, AD0, XCL, XDA, SDA, SCL, GND, VCC — the four you want are the four on the right-hand end, and they come in the same order as the Pico’s pins, which for once makes this easy.

Physical pinName on the boardSensor pad
1GP0SDA — the data
2GP1SCL — the clock
3GNDGND
363V3VCC — power in
Two wires can share one pad, and here they must Both the screen and the sensor need SDA. Both need SCL. So pin 1 ends up with two wires on it and so does pin 2, and that is not a mistake — it is the entire point of a bus. Wokwi will let you draw the second wire onto a pin that already has one.
VCC goes to 3V3, not to 5V Physical pin 36, the same one the screen uses. Pins 39 and 40 look like power and are 5 V — still the one rule this module has repeated since Activity 1.
3

You can move the sensor while it is running

This is the part of the simulator that makes today work without a board. Click the sensor part while the simulation is running and a little panel opens with sliders on it: three for acceleration, three for rotation, and one for temperature. Drag the acceleration sliders and your program sees the numbers change, exactly as if you had picked the board up.

Nothing to wire, again. The sensor is already joined to GP0 and GP1 inside the board, in copper, on the same two lines as the screen.

1

Plug in and power up

USB cable in, power switch SW5 on, and Thonny showing >>> in the Shell.

2

Get today’s two library files on

This is your wiring step. Go back to the library section and finish it before you write a line of the program.

3

Have somewhere to put the board down

You are going to be picking it up and tilting it with a USB cable attached. Leave enough slack, and keep the cable out of the way of your own hand.

The safety rules have not gone away Never connect a 5V pin to a GP pin — GP pins are 3.3 V only. Never join a power pin to a GND pin. Switch the board off at SW5 before changing any wires.

🚀 Exercise — a light that knows it has been tilted

A number on a screen has to be read. A light does not — you notice it from across the room, out of the corner of your eye, while doing something else.

Task: keep the program you have just written, screen and all, and add the red LED1. It comes on when the board has been tilted past halfway, and goes out when the board is put back down flat. The number on the screen carries on exactly as it does now.

  • The red LED is GP11 — the same pin, and the same two lines, as Activity 1
  • Tilted means the reading has got past 0.5. Do not ask whether it is exactly anything
  • The decision goes inside the loop, so it is taken again every half second
  • Ask the question of the whole reading. Rounding is for the screen, and only for the screen
  • Wokwi route: you will need to add an LED and a resistor — the wiring is below

The extra wiring, on the Wokwi route

On the real LilEx5 there is nothing to do: LED1 is soldered on GP11 already. In Wokwi you have to add it, and it is exactly the circuit you built in Activity 1.

The resistor is not optional Same rule as Activity 1: without it Wokwi shows a warning triangle, and a real LED burns out. Bands orange-orange-brown-gold, which is 330 Ω.
💡 Clue 1 — I don’t know where to start

Do not start a new file. Start from the program you have just written, which already reads the sensor and already writes to the screen, and give it one more thing to do with the reading it already has.

Above the loop, one line is added and nothing else changes: the LED needs a name, the same way it did in Activity 1 — and it needs to be told it is an output.

Inside the loop, the shape you want is Activity 5’s, unchanged: ask a question, do one thing when the answer is yes, and something else when it is no. The only thing that has moved on since Activity 5 is the question itself.

💡 Clue 2 — what question do I ask, and of what?

Two decisions to make, and they are both about the question rather than about the light.

The line at 0.5 is a choice, not a fact. Anything from about 0.4 to 0.8 will behave sensibly; a line at 0.99 will flicker, and a line at 0.05 will be on almost all the time.

💡 Clue 3 — nearly there

Five things to check before you decide it is broken:

  • Is the LED named at the top, and told it is an output? Without that second part the line runs and the light never comes on.
  • Is the decision inside the loop, at four spaces, with its two branches at eight? Outside the loop it is taken once, at the start, when the board is still flat.
  • Is there something for the no case? Without it the light comes on and never goes off again — nothing has been told to switch it back.
  • Are you asking about the reading, or about the rounded copy? Both work today; only one of them is the habit worth having.
  • Is the screen work still there, and still in the right order — wipe, write, show?

Test it by tilting slowly through the line and back. If the light comes on and stays on, the no case is missing. If it never comes on at all, tilt further and watch the number: it may simply never be getting past your line.

No answer on this page There is no answer block here on purpose — work it out from the clues, and check it by tilting the board rather than by reading. Your teacher has the worked answer if the class needs it.

🌟 Going further

Finished early? Try these — no answers given.

  1. Tilt the board the other way and watch the light stay dark. Work out why from the number on the screen, then make it come on for both directions. Activity 6 has the tool for joining two questions into one.
  2. Show X and Y on two labelled rows at once, so the screen says which way the board is leaning as well as how far.
  3. Use the buzzer on 14 instead of the light. It is set up exactly like an LED. Warn the person next to you first.
  4. Green when the board is flat, red when it is tilted. Two decisions, side by side, the way Activity 5’s exercise did it.
  5. Move the line from 0.5 to 0.9 and see how far you have to tip the board. Then try 0.99 and watch it flicker on the edge.
  6. Add Z on a third row. Predict what it will read while the board is flat before you run it, and write your prediction down.
  7. Change the rounding from one place to two, and then to none. Watch what happens to the width of the number and to how much of your movement survives.
  8. The sensor has a thermometer inside it as well, and it is spelt about as simply as you would guess. Find it, put it on a fourth row, and decide for yourself how much to trust it.
  9. There is a second set of three numbers, for how fast the board is turning rather than how it is tilted. It is spelt like the first set with one word changed. Find it, and notice that it drops back to nothing the moment you stop moving.

📖 Words to remember

SensorA part that measures something real and answers with how much, not yes or no.
AccelerometerThe sensor on this board. It notices which way gravity is pulling, which makes it a tilt meter when it is sitting still.
gThe unit it answers in: 1 g is the full strength of gravity. Flat on the desk, one direction reads about 1.
ReadingOne answer from a sensor, taken at one moment. Take it again and it will be slightly different.
Decimal placeOne digit after the point. 0.9 has one; 0.98 has two.
FloatWhat programmers call a number with a point in it, as opposed to a whole one. You will meet the word everywhere online.
round()Shortens a number to a given number of decimal places. What it throws away is gone for good.
>More than. The comparison a measurement needs: it asks which side of a line the reading has landed on, rather than for one exact value.
ThresholdThe line you choose to compare against. There is no right answer — pick one far away from where the reading normally sits.
str()Turns a number into the letters that spell it, so that it can be drawn on a screen.
Address 0x68The sensor’s number on the bus — 104 in ordinary numbers. The screen’s is 0x3C, which is 60.
DependencyA library that another library needs. imu.py depends on vector3d.py; both have to be on the Pico.
