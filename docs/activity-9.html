🎯 Your mission

Find out whether a plant needs watering without putting a finger in the soil — and have the board say so in a word a person can act on.

Two prongs go into the soil. A number comes back. That number means nothing on its own, so the program turns it into a percentage, and then into one of three words: DRY, JUST RIGHT or WET.

What you will learn

Analogue, and the ADC

A pin that answers with a position on a scale rather than a yes or a no — and the part inside the Pico that makes that possible.

Three roads, not two

Every decision so far had two ways out. Dry, just right and wet is three, and there is a word for that.

Whole-number division

A percentage does not want a tail of digits after the point. Two slashes instead of one, and the tail never appears.

Calibrating — the real one

No worksheet can tell you what your probe reads in your soil. You have to go and find out, and then the numbers are yours.

📈 Digital and analogue — the one new idea

Every pin you have used so far has been digital. It had two states and only two: on or off, 3.3 volts or 0 volts, 1 or 0. There was nothing in between because there was allowed to be nothing in between.

A button is honestly like that. It really is either pressed or not pressed. Soil is not. Soil is damp, or fairly damp, or nearly dry, or bone dry, and every shade between — and a pin that can only say yes or no has nothing useful to say about it.

Analogue is the word for the right-hand picture: an answer that can be anywhere on a scale. Sound is analogue. Temperature is analogue. How much water is in a cup of soil is analogue. Almost everything real is, and the two-state world of a button is the unusual one.

Neither one is better A button is digital because a button really only has two states, and pretending otherwise would be silly. Soil is analogue because it really does have every shade in between, and flattening that to yes-or-no throws the answer away. The kind of signal follows the thing being measured, not the other way round.

🔢 How the ADC works

Here is the awkward part. A computer cannot hold a voltage. It holds numbers — that is all it has ever been able to do. So somewhere between the wire and your program, the voltage has to become a number.

The part that does it is called an ADC, which is short for Analogue to Digital Converter. There are three of them inside the Pico, and they are wired to three particular pins. It takes the voltage on the pin and gives back a whole number: 0 V becomes 0, 3.3 V becomes 65535, and everything in between lands in between, in proportion.

65535 looks like an odd place to stop, and it is: it is the largest number that fits in sixteen binary digits. You do not need to know why to use it. What matters is that the scale never changes. The bottom is always 0 and the top is always 65535, whatever is plugged in.

One look, and then the pin carries on moving

The voltage on the pin does not wait for you. It drifts about the whole time the program is running. Asking the ADC for a number takes one look, right now, and hands you what it saw at that instant. Take another look a second later and it will be a slightly different number, because the world moved.

Only three pins can do this, and only up to 3.3 volts On the Pico they are GP26, GP27 and GP28. Asking any other pin to measure gives you Pin(x) doesn't have ADC capabilities. And the probe must be powered from 3V3, never from a 5 V pin — 5 V on a GP pin damages it. This is the same rule Activity 1 started with, and it is the one that costs money when it is broken.

💧 Why more water gives a smaller number

This is the one thing about today that everybody gets backwards, so here it is before you meet it: a big number means dry. Water the plant and the number goes down.

The probe is two metal prongs and nothing clever at all. It puts a voltage on one prong and looks at what arrives at the other. Dry soil is a poor conductor, so hardly any current flows and almost the whole voltage is still there — a high voltage, and therefore a big number. Wet soil is full of water carrying dissolved salts, which conducts rather well, so the voltage is pulled down. Low voltage, small number.

Say it out loud once and it stops catching you Big number, dry soil. Small number, wet soil. Everything else today follows from that one sentence — including why the dry threshold is the bigger of your two numbers.

🚩 Three zones, and the word for them — the second new idea

Every decision you have written has had two ways out. Pressed or not pressed. Above the line or below it. if and else, and that was the whole story.

Soil has three answers worth giving. Too dry, and the plant needs water. Too wet, and it is drowning. In between, leave it alone — and leave it alone is genuinely different from the other two, not a shade of either. Two roads will not carry three destinations.

Where the three zones come from

You pick two numbers, and the two numbers cut the whole range into three pieces. That is all a zone is. The names dry and wet are just variables, exactly like every variable since Activity 1.

Two roads became three

The new word is elif — a squashed-up spelling of else if. It goes between the if and the else, and it means “that first question was a no; here is a second question”.

The important part is not the spelling. It is that the questions are asked in order and stop at the first yes. As soon as one of them is true, its lines run and the rest are not even looked at. Exactly one of the three things happens, every single time round the loop — never two, and never none.

The order matters, and getting it wrong is silent The second question is only ever asked when the first said no, so it does not have to rule out the first case again. If the two questions are swapped round, the program still runs perfectly and every dry reading is reported as just right — because above the wet number is true for dry soil as well. No error, wrong answer. Keep the biggest number first.
Still one decision, still one indent if, elif and else all line up at the same left edge, because they are three parts of one decision. What each of them does is indented under it, the way it has been since Activity 5.

📋 Turning the raw number into a percentage

55693 is a true answer. It is not a useful one. Nobody has ever watered a plant because the number was 55693.

A percentage is useful because everybody already knows what it means. The sum that gets you there has four parts, and none of them is difficult on its own.

Why two slashes and not one

One slash is ordinary division and it hands back the whole truth, tail and all. Two slashes throw the tail away and give a whole number. A percentage has no use for the tail, so two slashes it is.

And what about readings outside your two numbers? Soil drier than your dry number gives a negative percentage. Soil wetter than your wet number goes past 100. Both are honest answers to the sum and both look broken on a screen, so the program has two short lines that pull them back to 0 and 100. Let somebody in the class hit 138 % before you fix it — it is much more memorable that way round.

🌡️ Now calibrate it — this is the real lesson

If your soil says DRY when it is plainly soaking, or JUST RIGHT the moment you plug it in, nothing is broken. The two numbers in your program are somebody else’s guess, and now you are going to replace them with measurements.

50000 and 25000 were written by a person who has never met your probe, your soil or your cup. A different probe, older or newer, reads differently. Sandy soil reads differently from compost. A prong pushed in deeper reads differently from the same prong pushed in half way. No worksheet anywhere can know your numbers, which is why you have to go and find them.

That is what calibrating means, and it is not special to soil. Every instrument that has ever measured anything was calibrated by somebody first, against something known. Today the two known things are as dry as it will ever be and as wet as it will ever be.

Choose your two numbers, just inside the ends

Put dry a little below the air reading, and wet a little above the soaked one. Roughly a twentieth of the way in at each end is plenty. Sitting exactly on the ends is a bad idea — readings wobble, and a threshold right on the edge flickers.

Then go back to your program, change the two lines, and run it again.

The Wokwi route gives you a free check In the simulator the air reading is exactly 65535 and soaked is exactly 0, because the simulated sensor uses the whole scale. Put those two in as your numbers and something rather satisfying happens: the percentage your program prints becomes the same number as the water slider. Set the slider to 40 and the program says 40. If it does, every part of your sum is right.
The awkward question, and it is worth asking out loud What happens if dry and wet end up the wrong way round — the dry number smaller than the wet one? Try it before you decide. There is no error, the program runs perfectly, and everything it tells you is wrong. Working out why from the number line above is worth more than being told.
Different soil, different numbers — and that is fine Two groups sitting next to each other will not have the same two numbers, and neither of them is mistaken. The numbers belong to the probe and the cup, not to the activity. Write them on a sticky note and keep them with the probe.

🚀 Exercise — warn me without words

Nobody watches a screen all day, and nobody reads a Shell at all once they have left the room. A light you notice from the doorway.

Task: keep the program exactly as it is, printing and all, and add two LEDs. The red one comes on when the soil is dry. The green one comes on when it is wet. When it is just right, neither is lit — which is the answer you want to see from across the room.

  • Red is GP11 and green is GP13 — the same two pins, and the same two lines, as Activity 1. The same numbers on both routes, so the program does not change between them
  • The three branches already exist. You are adding lines inside them, not writing a new decision
  • Each branch has to deal with both lights, not just its own
  • Wokwi route: you will need two LEDs and two 330 Ω resistors — the wiring is below

💡 Clue 1 — where the new lines go

Two lines go at the top of the program, above the loop, next to where soil is set up: one to name the red light and one to name the green, each told it is an output. That is Activity 1, unchanged.

Everything else goes inside the three branches you already have. You are not writing a fourth question, and you are not writing a second decision underneath the first. The decision that already sorts the soil into three zones is the same decision that decides which light is on.

💡 Clue 2 — each branch has two jobs

A light does not switch itself off. If the dry branch only ever turns the red one on, then the first time the soil goes dry the red light comes on and stays on for the rest of the afternoon, whatever happens to the soil afterwards.

So every branch has to say what both lights are doing — including the middle one, where the answer is that neither of them is doing anything.

The print() lines stay exactly where they are. You are adding to each branch, not replacing what is in it.

💡 Clue 3 — nearly there

Five things to check before you decide it is broken:

  • Are both lights named at the top, and both told they are outputs? Without the second part the lines run and nothing lights.
  • Are the new lines at eight spaces, under their branch — not at four, alongside elif?
  • Does the middle branch turn both off? It is the one people leave empty, and it is the reason a light gets stuck on.
  • Are the numbers 11 and 13, not 1 and 3, and not the physical pin numbers off the diagram?
  • Is the whole thing still inside the loop, so it is decided again every second?

Test it by watering slowly and watching for the moment the red goes out. If a light comes on and stays on whatever you do, a branch is missing an instruction. If both are on together, one branch is turning something on and no branch is turning it off.

No answer on this page There is no answer block here on purpose — work it out from the clues, and check it by watering the soil rather than by reading. Your teacher has the worked answer if the class needs it.

🌟 Going further

Finished early? Try these — no answers given.

  1. Add the buzzer on GP14 to the dry branch as well as the light. It is set up exactly like an LED. Warn the person next to you first.
  2. Make the red light blink instead of sitting on, so it is harder to ignore. Activity 2 has everything you need and nothing new is required.
  3. Put the percentage on the screen from Activity 7, so the board says how thirsty rather than just that it is thirsty. Remember what Activity 8 said about numbers and words.
  4. Ask about the percentage instead of the raw reading. The decision can be written either way. Which one is easier to read six months later, and which one is easier to get backwards?
  5. Send it to the internet with Activity 10, and watch a graph of your cup of soil drying out over a whole weekend.
  6. Add a second probe on GP27 and compare two cups — one watered, one not. Two probes need two sets of calibration numbers, which is the whole lesson again from the other side.
  7. Take three readings and average them before deciding anything, so a single odd reading cannot flip the light. Whole-number division is already in your program.
  8. Move your two numbers closer together until JUST RIGHT is a very narrow band, and watch the verdict flicker. Then work out what that tells you about choosing a threshold.
