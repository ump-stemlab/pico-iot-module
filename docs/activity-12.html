Sending Messages by Radio

Yesterday your board sent a word across the world, and it went through somebody else’s computer to get there. Today it sends one across the room, and there is nothing in between but air. No server, no account, no internet, and no permission from anybody.

🎯 Your mission

Catch what your instructor is broadcasting. Then move your radio onto a different channel, and answer back.

Three short programs, none of them longer than a page. The first one listens on channel 23. The second moves your radio to channel 24 and stops. The third sends your own message out on 24, where your instructor is waiting for it.

What you will learn

🔄 UART

The oldest and simplest way for two chips to talk: one wire out, one wire in, one bit at a time.

🎛️ Mode pins

Three ordinary output pins that tell the radio what job to do — including whether to wake up at all.

📡 Channels

A number that picks a lane in the air. Everybody on the same number hears everybody else.

💬 encode and decode

A radio carries numbers. Letters are a human idea, so words go out as bytes and come back as words.

What you are reusing today, and will not be taught again The loop and sleep (Activity 2), Pin and comments (Activity 1), print() (Activity 3), if (Activity 5), joining text with + and str() (Activity 7), def (Activity 11), and turning bytes back into words with decode() (Activity 11). All of it comes back today, and none of it is explained again.

🚫 Nobody in the middle — the one new idea

In Activity 11 a click on a web page turned on your LED. It worked, and it worked from anywhere — but count the things that had to be awake for it to happen.

A WiFi network. An account. A key. A server in a data centre somewhere, owned by a company you have never met, which had to still be running at that exact moment. Take away any one of them and the light does not move. Today all of them are gone, and the message still arrives.

This is why it matters, and it is not a toy A wireless doorbell, a car key, a farm sensor two fields away from the nearest wire, a walkie-talkie, a weather balloon: none of them can assume there is a network. A link like today’s keeps working in a power cut, in a field, and in a country whose internet has been switched off.
What you give up, and you should know it before you start There is no acknowledgement: your board never finds out whether anybody heard it. There is no ordering: two messages can arrive the wrong way round. There is no address today: everybody on your channel gets everything. The server you have just thrown away was doing all three of those jobs for you.

🧪 No simulator today

Wokwi can pretend to be a Pico, a screen, a button and a sensor. It cannot pretend to be a radio.

Every activity so far has had two routes, and you have always started in the simulator. This one has one route. There is no project to open and nothing to click play on — everything today happens on the real boards, in the real room, with real air in between. That is awkward, and it is also the point: the thing you are learning about is the air.

Check the antenna is screwed on before anybody presses Run A radio with no antenna cannot get its signal out — and it can damage itself trying to. Look at your board now, and look at your neighbour’s.

What you need

🔌 Your LilEx5 board

Pico W, USB cable, antenna screwed on. The radio is already on the board.

💻 Thonny

Where you write the three short programs, exactly as in every activity since Activity 0.

👨‍🏫 Your instructor’s two boards

One broadcasts on 23. The other listens on 24, on the projector, and is the only proof you get that Part 3 worked.

🚫 Nothing to wire

Not one jumper wire. The wiring was done at the factory, in tracks inside the green plastic.

🔄 How the Pico talks to the radio

The Pico cannot make a radio wave. It hands the message to a chip that can — and the way it hands it over is called UART.

You have met a bus like this before. In Activity 7 the screen was on I²C, where several parts share two wires and each one has an address. UART is simpler and older: two chips, two wires, and no addresses at all. One wire carries bytes out; the other carries bytes in. Nobody else is on it.

Baud rate, in one sentence How many bits go down the wire each second. Both ends have to have agreed beforehand, because nothing in the signal says how fast it is going. Set one end to a different number and you get letters, but the wrong ones — which is exactly what strange symbols in the Shell mean.

🎛️ The three mode pins — do this first or nothing works

M0, M1 and M2 together tell the radio what job to do. There are eight combinations and you will use two of them.

They are ordinary output pins, set exactly the way you set an LED in Activity 1. What makes them worth a section of their own is what happens if you skip them.

A radio nobody has spoken to is fast asleep Small resistors on the board pull all three pins up to 1, and 1, 1, 1 is sleep. So every one of today’s programs starts the same way: three Pin lines and three value lines. Miss them and nothing will ever arrive — and there is no error message to tell you why. The Shell just stays empty and you think the radio is broken.

▶️ Run it, and wait

Press the green ▶ Run arrow, or F5. One line appears. Then the Shell goes quiet — until your instructor presses Run.

Nobody is being sent to individually One board transmits and everybody within range hears it. That is what broadcast means, and it is how ordinary radio has always worked — a transmitter does not know, and cannot know, who is listening.

📡 Part 2 — change lane

If the whole class answered on channel 23, thirty people would be shouting over the instructor. So everybody moves to 24.

A channel is just a number

0 to 31. The frequency is 410 MHz plus the channel number, so channel 23 is 433 MHz and channel 24 is 434 MHz. That is the whole idea — a lane in the air, picked by a number.

Radio is regulated, and this is not a detail You may not transmit on any frequency you fancy. The two channels you are using sit in a band that is open to everybody, worldwide, for low-power devices — the same band as garage-door remotes. Every other channel on this module belongs to somebody who was given it. Do not go looking.

The program that moves you

This one sends no message at all. It changes one setting inside the radio and stops. Save it as channel.py and run it once.

Code is shown as a picture — turn on JavaScript to see it.

What it is actually doing

  1. Mode pins to 1, 1, 0 — the radio now listens to the Pico instead of to the air.
  2. Ask the radio what it is set to. Six bytes come back.
  3. Copy them, change two of them, hand them back.
  4. Mode pins back to 1, 0, 0, and stop.

The six bytes

Everything the radio knows about itself is kept in six numbers. Read them, change one, hand them back.

Two bits of notation you have not met before 0xC1 is a number written in hexadecimal — it is 193, in a way that is easier to read a byte in. And the number in square brackets picks one item out of the six: box 0 is the first, box 4 is the fifth. Counting from zero is normal everywhere in programming and it does catch people once.
Run this once, and read the two lines it prints If the first line says None, the radio did not answer, which means it is not in settings mode — check the mode line before you change anything else. Do not just run it again and hope.
It survives the power going off, and that is deliberate Byte 0 was set to the save for good command. Your board will still be on channel 24 next week. Your instructor has a one-line change that puts it back on 23 — ask, do not guess.

Why it arrives jumbled — and what a collision is

Thirty boards are sharing one channel, and none of them asks permission. Most of the time the messages simply interleave. Sometimes two boards transmit at the same instant, the two signals land on top of each other, and neither one gets through. That is a collision, and nobody is told it happened.

A room of people solves this the same way Not by shouting louder. By taking turns, or by somebody saying who speaks next. Real radio systems do exactly that too — and every one of them had to be invented, because the air itself does not do it for you.

🚀 Exercise — count what you catch

Your instructor is going to broadcast a burst of messages on channel 23. How many did you actually get?

Task: change your listening program so that it counts the messages and prints the number in front of every one.

  • Nothing about the radio changes. Not the mode pins, not the link, not the channel. This is a counting problem
  • You have already written the counting line once today — it is in the sending program
  • Start from your listening program, not from a blank file

What success looks like

💡 Clue 1 — where the counter goes

One new line goes above the loop, next to where the link is opened. It makes a variable and puts 0 in it. That is Activity 1, unchanged: a name and a value.

It has to be above the loop. If it is inside, it is set back to 0 every time round and your count never gets past 1 — which is a real thing that happens, and it looks like the counting line is broken when it is not.

💡 Clue 2 — when to add one

The loop goes round about ten times a second whether anything arrived or not. If you add one every time round, you will be counting trips round the loop, not messages, and the number will race away while the Shell stays quiet.

So the counting line goes inside the if, at eight spaces, in among the lines that only run when something has actually arrived. The exact line already exists in your sending program — go and look at it.

💡 Clue 3 — nearly there

Four things to check before you decide it is broken:

  • Is the counter created above the loop, at no indent at all?
  • Is the adding line at eight spaces, under the if, and not at four alongside it?
  • Does the printing line mention the counter as well as the message? print() will join a number to text for you if you separate them with commas — Activity 4’s trick.
  • Is the number going up by one per message, or by one per trip round the loop? If it jumps to four hundred while nothing is arriving, the adding line is in the wrong place.

Test it by asking your instructor to send exactly five, and counting them yourself as they land.

🌟 Going further

Finished early? Try these — no answers given.

  1. Flash the green LED on GP13 every time a message arrives, so you can count them from across the room. Activity 2 has everything you need.
  2. Put the message on the OLED screen from Activity 7, so the board does not need a computer attached to be useful.
  3. Print only the messages with your own name in them. One if, and a question you have not asked before — find out how to ask whether one piece of text is inside another.
  4. Send a soil reading instead of a greeting, using Activity 9. A moisture sensor in a field with no WiFi is the actual reason this radio is on the board.
  5. Make the sending program wait one second instead of five, then get the whole row to run it at once. Watch what happens to the instructor’s screen, and work out why.
  6. Give two boards a private line. Bytes 1 and 2 of the settings are an address, and two radios only hear each other if their addresses match as well as their channels. Your teacher has the details.

📖 Words to remember

RadioSending information as a wave through the air, with no wire between the two ends.
UARTA two-wire serial link between two chips: one wire out, one wire in, one bit at a time. No addresses, and only two ends.
Baud rateHow fast those two wires talk. Both ends must have agreed beforehand — ours use 9600.
Mode pinsThree ordinary output pins that tell the radio what job to do. 1, 1, 1 is asleep, and that is where every board starts.
ChannelA number that picks the frequency. Here the frequency is 410 plus the channel, so 23 is 433 MHz.
BroadcastTransmitting to everybody in range at once, without knowing or caring who is listening.
CollisionTwo radios transmitting at the same instant, so neither message gets through cleanly — and nobody is told.
encode / decodeTurning text into bytes, and bytes back into text. A radio carries numbers; letters are a human idea.
HexadecimalCounting in sixteens instead of tens, written with a 0x in front. A convenient way to write one byte.
