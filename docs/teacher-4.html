Activity 4 — teacher notes

Making Decisions. Everything on this page is for the person running the lesson.

▶ Open the student page ← Activity 3 notes 📌 Pin reference
Keep this link to yourself. The page is unlisted and hidden from search engines, but the repository is public — anyone given the URL can read it. Share it with staff, not in the student handout.

What is actually being taught

One idea: a program can choose. Everything before this activity ran the same way every time; from here on a program's path depends on what is happening in the world. It is the largest conceptual step in the module and everything after it assumes the step has been taken.

The idea arrives in two halves, deliberately separated. if first, with a demonstration that it leaves the light stuck on; then else as the fix. Do not short-circuit this by writing both at once on the board. The class needs to see the light that will not go out, or else is just a word they copied.

Two traps carry the activity, and they are not equally dangerous:

  • The double equals is the headline trap and the one the page gives most room to — but it is the kind one. A single equals is a SyntaxError, so the program refuses to start and the message points at the line. Nobody loses a lesson to it.
  • The comparison itself is the dangerous one. == 1 instead of == 0 runs perfectly and does the exact opposite. This is Activity 3's inversion finally collecting its debt, and a student who never really accepted “0 means pressed” will write it, see a light that is on except when they press, and have no idea why. Send them back to Activity 3's section rather than re-explaining it — the two pull-up diagrams are the argument.
The question to open with “Last lesson you all wanted the button to turn the light on and I said no. Why could we not do it?” Push until somebody says something like “it didn't know how to only sometimes do it”. That sentence is the whole activity, and it is much better coming from them.
No and, or or not today That is Activity 5. One button, one condition, all the way through — including the exercise, which is deliberately two separate decisions rather than one combined one. If a student produces a combined condition, it works and it is genuinely clever; praise it, and ask them to also write the two-block version so they have both, because Activity 5 builds on the contrast.

The main program

Code is shown as a picture — turn on JavaScript to see it.

Nine working lines, five of which are shapes they have already typed. The comments are optional as always — do not mark a student down for a program without them.

Why print stays in The lights alone would be enough. The printing lines are there because they split one confusing symptom into two separate diagnoses: if the words are right and the light is wrong, the fault is in the wiring; if the words are wrong too, the fault is in the decision. That is a genuinely useful debugging habit and this is the first activity where it can be demonstrated.
0.1 rather than 0.2 Activity 3 used a fifth of a second to keep the console readable. Here the loop is driving a light that has to keep up with a finger, so it is a tenth. Worth saying out loud that the number is chosen for the job, not remembered from last time — and that with the light, unlike the console, a slightly missed tap still leaves a visible flash.

Exercise answer — two decisions at once

Code is shown as a picture — turn on JavaScript to see it.

The exercise needs one thing from Activity 1 (each part gets its own name) and one thing from today, used twice. There is no new syntax in it at all, which is the point: the shape scales by repetition, not by cleverness.

Accept any sensible names. Three things worth insisting on:

  • Two separate blocks, not one clever condition. The two decisions must know nothing about each other. If they are entangled, the four-state test in the diagram will catch it — ask the student to press both buttons at once.
  • The second if at four spaces. Putting it at eight makes it part of the first decision, so SW2 only works while SW1 is held down. It is the most interesting wrong answer in the exercise and worth demonstrating on the projector.
  • One waiting line, at the bottom, at four spaces. Two waits halve the response rate for no benefit; a wait inside a block makes the loop's speed depend on which road was taken.
If a pair finishes very early Ask them to make SW1 turn the green light off as well as turning the red one on. They will need two lines inside one if, which is the first time a block has held more than one line — and it makes the indentation rule pay for itself.
Wokwi needs a second LED and a second resistor Green on GP13 with its own 330 Ω, and a second pushbutton on GP3. That is four parts and eight wires on the diagram; expect the wiring, not the code, to be what slows the Wokwi route down. Pairs on the real board have nothing to wire and will finish noticeably sooner — have the going-further list ready for them.

Going-further answers

TaskWhat happens / what to say
Add SW3 on 4 and LED2 on 12 A third block, identical in shape. Nothing new — which is exactly the observation you want them to make out loud.
Make SW2 work the other way roundSwap the two lines inside the block, or compare with 1. Both are correct. The second is more interesting, because it is the trap from earlier in the lesson used on purpose — a good moment to point out that nothing was ever wrong with == 1, only with using it when you meant == 0.
Buzzer BZ1 on 14Set up exactly like an LED and driven exactly like one. Loud. Ten seconds is plenty for the demonstration.
SW1 lights red and switches green offTwo lines inside one block, both at eight spaces. This is the answer to “how many lines can go inside an if” — as many as you like, as long as they all line up.
Slide switch SW4 on 15 chooses which lightA decision inside a decision — twelve spaces. It works, and it is the natural point to say that Activity 5 gives them a tidier way of expressing the same thing. Do not require it.
Take the wait outNothing visible changes. The light is already following the finger faster than an eye can see, and unlike Activity 2's blink there is nothing being switched on and off rapidly. Good prediction question — most of the class will expect a flicker.

Before the lesson

  • Activities 1 to 3 must be done. Naming an output pin, naming an input pin, the forever-loop, stopping it, and value() are all assumed and none is re-taught.
  • Check that the class actually accepted the inversion. Two minutes at the start: everyone presses a button and says what number they would see. If a third of the room says 1, teach that before you teach if, or the lesson will fall apart at the comparison.
  • A LilEx5 board and USB cable per pair; check SW1 clicks and LED1 lights on each board.
  • Thonny installed, interpreter preset to MicroPython (Raspberry Pi Pico).
  • For the Wokwi route, budget extra time for wiring: this is the first circuit with two independent parts on it, and the exercise doubles that again.
  • The pin reference open on every machine. Buttons 2, 3, 4; LEDs 11, 12, 13.

The mistakes students actually make

What they didWhat they seeWhat to say
One equals sign in the questionSyntaxError: invalid syntax The most common error of the lesson and the least harmful. “One equals puts, two equals asks.” They will do it three or four times and then stop for good.
Compared with 1The light is on except while they pressThe dangerous one. Do not fix the line for them. Ask them to hold the button and say the number out loud, then read their own if line back. Send them to Activity 3's section if the penny does not drop.
No elseLight comes on once, never goes offExactly the demonstration the page asks for. Worth letting happen rather than preventing.
else at eight spacesSyntaxError on the else lineThey pressed Enter and let the editor indent for them. Backspace once. Point at the if above it: those two words line up.
Nothing indented under the ifIndentationError: expected an indented blockThe colon promises something underneath. Same contract as while in Activity 2.
A question after elseSyntaxErrorThey wrote the opposite question out longhand. else already means “every other case”. This is a good instinct badly aimed — and it is elif, which this module does not need.

Teaching notes

  • Build it in two goes, in front of them. Write the if half, run it, press the button, and let the light sit there refusing to go out. Ask what is missing. Then add else. Three minutes, and it is worth more than any explanation of what else means.
  • The decision widget on the student page is worth four minutes of class time. Put it on the projector and hold the pad: the pin value, the answer, the road and the light all move together. Then press Compare with 1 and hold it again — the trap, live, with nothing to type. Then Take the else away. Those two clicks replace a lot of talking.
  • Say “one equals puts, two equals asks” out loud and make them say it back. It sounds childish and it works. The SyntaxError will do the rest of the teaching.
  • Draw the fork on the whiteboard as a real fork in a road, with a signpost. Walk your finger down it. Students who are shaky on this are usually imagining both roads being taken, or neither; the physical gesture of choosing one fixes it faster than a diagram they read.
  • Indentation now has two levels and that is new. Some students will indent everything by eight, including else, on the grounds that it is all “inside” something. Ask them inside what, for each line, one at a time.
  • The word condition is introduced in the vocabulary table but not leaned on. Use it if the class is comfortable; “the question” is fine and is what the page says everywhere else.
  • Resist elif. It will come up from someone who has looked things up. It is not needed anywhere in this module and it makes the two-road picture harder to hold on to.
  • The safety rules still apply: never join 5V to a GP pin, never join a power pin to GND, switch off at SW5 before changing wires.
