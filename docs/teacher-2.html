Activity 2 — teacher notes

Make an LED Blink. Everything on this page is for the person running the lesson.

Keep this link to yourself. The page is unlisted and hidden from search engines, but the repository is public — anyone given the URL can read it. Share it with staff, not in the student handout.

What is actually being taught

One idea: a block of code can repeat. Everything else on the student page — the loop, the pause, the four spaces — is machinery in service of that.

The blink itself is easy. Indentation is the lesson. It is the first time in this module that whitespace changes meaning, and it is the single thing most of your class will get wrong at least once. Budget time for it and do not rush past the two comparison diagrams.

The question to ask before they type anything “In Activity 1 your light stayed on. What would it take to make it go off again?” Let them answer “tell it to switch off”, then ask why adding off at the end of Activity 1's program does not appear to work. That is the whole activity, in one question.

Exercise answer — the traffic light

Code is shown as a picture — turn on JavaScript to see it.

Three groups of three inside one loop. The exercise deliberately needs two things from Activity 1 (each LED gets its own name) and two things from this one (the loop, and a different waiting time for the yellow), so it is a genuine check that Activity 1 stuck.

Accept any sensible order and any sensible names. The three things worth insisting on:

  • Every colour is switched off again before the next one comes on. Students who only ever switch on end up with all three lit — a lovely, obvious failure.
  • All nine lines are indented. One group left at the left edge is the classic mistake, and the symptom is subtle: that colour appears to be skipped.
  • The yellow really is shorter. If all three delays are the same they have not read the task.

Blank lines between the groups are worth praising out loud — it is the first time formatting for a human reader has come up since comments in Activity 1.

If a pair finishes very early Ask them to make the sequence twice as fast by editing one line only. They cannot yet — unless they invent a variable for the delay, which is going-further task 3 and a genuinely elegant answer for a beginner. It also pre-teaches why named values matter, which pays off in Activity 7.

Going-further answers

TaskWhat happens / what to say
Yellow blinks three timesSix more lines inside the loop: on, wait, off, wait — three times over. Some students will ask “is there a shorter way?” There is (for), and it is not in this module — tell them the instinct is exactly right and that counted loops are the next thing they would learn.
Red and yellow togetherTwo on lines with no off between them, then a wait, then two off lines. Proves that a pin holds its state until something changes it.
Name the delayA line such as a nickname set to 4 above the loop, then that name used instead of the number. Editing one line changes the whole light. This is the best answer of the five and worth showing to the class.
A waiting time of 0No error. The pause is real but effectively instantaneous, so that colour flashes for a few microseconds and looks like it never came on. Same lesson as the too-fast preset on the student page.
Buzzer on 14A short beep every cycle. Loud in a full lab — consider demonstrating from the front rather than letting thirty pairs discover it at once. Also a useful reminder that the buzzer is switched exactly like an LED.

Before the lesson

  • Activity 1 must be done. This activity does not re-teach the wiring, the pin numbers or variables.
  • A LilEx5 board and USB cable per pair; check SW5 works on each.
  • Thonny installed, interpreter preset to MicroPython (Raspberry Pi Pico).
  • Have Wokwi open on the projector. If your class saved their Activity 1 projects, remind them to reopen rather than rebuild.
  • Know where the Stop button is on both tools before you start. You will be asked within five minutes, by everyone, at the same time.
  • The pin reference open on every machine.

The mistakes students actually make

What they didWhat they seeWhat to say
The off lines are not indentedThe LED comes on and stays on. No error. The big one. Ask them to point at which lines are inside the loop. They usually spot it themselves once asked to say it out loud rather than look at it.
Nothing indented under whileIndentationError: expected an indented block Easy — the message is clear and names the line. A loop must contain something.
Missing colon on the while lineSyntaxError The editor stops auto-indenting too, so the whole block collapses to the left edge and looks doubly broken. Fix the colon first, then re-indent.
Lowercase trueNameError or SyntaxError Same lesson as lowercase pin in Activity 1. The type-it-here box catches it.
No sleep importNameError: name 'sleep' is not defined They remember to borrow Pin and forget the second toolbox. Point at line 2 of their own program.
Only one sleep, after the onThe LED looks permanently on, very slightly dim.Genuinely hard to see. Slow it right down — five seconds on, five off — and the missing dark half becomes obvious.
Tabs mixed with spacesTabError, or lines that look aligned but are not Rare in Thonny, more common if they paste from somewhere. Delete the leading whitespace and retype it.
Cannot save; Thonny appears frozenNo >>> in the Shell The old forever-loop is still running. Stop button, or Ctrl+C in the Shell. Expect this repeatedly — it is new this activity.

Teaching notes

  • Teach stopping before you teach starting. Show the Stop button and Ctrl+C on the projector before anyone runs a forever-loop, or you will spend the next ten minutes walking round doing it for them.
  • The blink widget on the student page is worth two minutes of class time. Put the “too fast” preset up on the projector and ask what the LED is doing. Nearly everyone says “it is on”. It is not — it is blinking fifty times a second. That single demonstration does more for the sleep idea than any explanation.
  • Indentation deserves a physical demonstration. Put the program on the board and physically drag one line left, then ask what changes. Do not accept “it breaks” — make them say which lines now repeat.
  • Still no print(). As in Activity 1, it is held back until Activity 3 where reading a button gives it a real job. A blinking light is its own output. If a student adds one, praise it and move on.
  • while True is not explained in terms of boolean logic here, on purpose. “True means keep going” is enough. True and False get their proper treatment in Activities 4 and 5.
  • The module borrows single tools (from time import sleep) rather than whole modules, so every activity's import lines look the same. The student page shows the other style in a details block so nobody is thrown by code they find online.
  • The waiting numbers are the easiest thing in the module to experiment with. Encourage fiddling — nothing can break, and the connection between a number in a file and a thing happening in the physical world is the point of the whole course.
  • The safety rules still apply: never join 5V to a GP pin, never join a power pin to GND, switch off at SW5 before changing wires.
