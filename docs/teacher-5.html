Activity 5 — teacher notes

Making Decisions. One idea: a program can choose.

▶ Student page 📚 All teacher notes ← Activity 4 notes 📌 Pin reference
Keep this link to yourself. Unlisted and hidden from search engines, but the repository is public — anyone with the URL can read it.

What is being taught

  • One idea: a program can choose. Everything before this ran the same way every time. The largest conceptual step in the module, and everything after it assumes the step was taken.
  • It arrives in two halves and the order matters. if first, with a demonstration that it leaves the light stuck on; then else as the fix. Do not write both at once on the board — the class needs to see the light that will not go out.
  • print() stays in the main program as a debugging tool: words right + light wrong means the wiring; words wrong too means the decision.
  • The loop waits 0.1, chosen for driving a light rather than throttling a console. Worth saying out loud that the number fits the job.
TrapBehaviourHow dangerous
One equals in a questionSyntaxError, nothing runs The headline trap, and the kind one. Nobody loses a lesson to it.
== 1 instead of == 0Runs perfectly, does the exact oppositeDangerous. Activity 4’s inversion collecting its debt. Send them to that section rather than re-explaining.
The question to open with “Last lesson you all wanted the button to turn the light on and I said no. Why could we not do it?” Push until somebody says “it didn’t know how to only sometimes do it”. That sentence is the whole activity, and it is far better coming from them.
No and, or or not today That is Activity 6. One button, one condition throughout — including the exercise, which is deliberately two separate decisions. If a student produces a combined condition it is genuinely clever: praise it, then ask for the two-block version too, because Activity 6 builds on the contrast.

The main program

# Three tools: Pin for the button and the light, sleep for the pause
from machine import Pin
from time import sleep

# SW1 is a button on GP2, held up until you press it
sw = Pin(2, Pin.IN, Pin.PULL_UP)

# LED1 is a light on GP11
led = Pin(11, Pin.OUT)

# Read the button, decide, act - over and over
while True:
    if sw.value() == 0:       # 0 means the button is pressed
        led.on()              # so switch the light on
        print("pressed")
    else:                     # anything else means it is not
        led.off()             # so switch the light off
        print("not pressed")
    sleep(0.1)

Nine working lines, five of them shapes they have already typed. Comments optional as always.

Exercise answer 
from machine import Pin
from time import sleep

# Two buttons and two lights, each with its own name
sw1 = Pin(2, Pin.IN, Pin.PULL_UP)
sw2 = Pin(3, Pin.IN, Pin.PULL_UP)
red = Pin(11, Pin.OUT)
green = Pin(13, Pin.OUT)

# Two decisions, one after the other, over and over
while True:
    if sw1.value() == 0:      # is SW1 down?
        red.on()
    else:
        red.off()

    if sw2.value() == 0:      # is SW2 down?
        green.on()
    else:
        green.off()

    sleep(0.1)

One thing from Activity 1 (each part gets its own name) and one from today, used twice. No new syntax at all — the shape scales by repetition, not cleverness. Accept any sensible names; insist on three things:

  • Two separate blocks, not one clever condition. The decisions must know nothing about each other. Ask the student to press both buttons at once — the four-state diagram is the acceptance test.
  • The second if at four spaces. At eight it becomes part of the first decision, so SW2 only works while SW1 is held. The most instructive wrong answer in the exercise — worth putting on the projector.
  • One waiting line, at the bottom, at four spaces. Two waits halve the response rate; a wait inside a block makes the loop speed depend on which road was taken.
If a pair finishes very early Ask them to make SW1 turn the green light off as well as the red one on. Two lines inside one if — the first time a block has held more than one line, and it makes the indentation rule pay for itself.
Wokwi needs a second LED and a second resistor Green on GP13 with its own 330 Ω, and a second pushbutton on GP3: four parts, eight wires. Expect the wiring, not the code, to slow the Wokwi route down. Real-board pairs finish much sooner — have the going-further list ready.

Going-further answers

TaskAnswer / what to say
Add SW3 on 4 and LED2 on 12 A third block, identical in shape. Nothing new — exactly the observation you want them to make out loud.
Make SW2 work the other way roundSwap the two lines inside the block, or compare with 1. Both correct; the second is more interesting, because it uses the trap on purpose. Nothing was ever wrong with == 1, only with using it when you meant == 0.
Buzzer BZ1 on 14Set up and driven exactly like an LED. Loud; ten seconds is plenty.
SW1 lights red and switches green offTwo lines inside one block, both at eight spaces. The answer to “how many lines can go inside an if”: as many as you like, as long as they line up.
SW4 on 15 chooses which lightA decision inside a decision — twelve spaces. It works, and it is the natural moment to say Activity 6 has a tidier way of saying the same thing. Do not require it.
Take the wait outNothing visible changes — the light already follows the finger faster than an eye can see. Good prediction question; most of the class expects a flicker.

Before the lesson

  • Activities 1–4 done. Naming pins, the loop, stopping it and value() are all assumed and none re-taught.
  • Check the class actually accepted the inversion. Two minutes at the start: everyone presses a button and says what number they would see. If a third of the room says 1, fix that before you teach if, or the lesson falls apart at the comparison.
  • A board and USB cable per pair; check SW1 clicks and LED1 lights on each.
  • Thonny installed, interpreter preset to MicroPython (Raspberry Pi Pico).
  • Wokwi route: budget extra time for wiring — first circuit with two independent parts, and the exercise doubles it.
  • The pin reference open on every machine. Buttons 2, 3, 4; LEDs 11, 12, 13.

Teaching notes

  • Build it in two goes, in front of them. Write the if half, run it, press the button, let the light refuse to go out. Ask what is missing. Then add else. Three minutes, worth more than any explanation.
  • The decision widget is worth four minutes on the projector. Hold the pad: pin value, answer, road and light all move together. Then Compare with 1, then Take the else away. Two clicks replace a lot of talking.
  • “One equals puts, two equals asks.” Say it, make them say it back. It sounds childish and it works.
  • Draw the fork as a real fork in a road, with a signpost, and walk your finger down it. Students who are shaky are usually imagining both roads or neither.
  • Two indent levels is new. Some will indent everything by eight, including else, because it is all “inside” something. Ask inside what, line by line.
  • The word condition is in the vocabulary table but not leaned on. “The question” is what the page says everywhere else.
  • Resist elif. It arrives in Activity 9, where three zones make it worth having; here it only makes the two-road picture harder to hold.
  • Safety rules unchanged: never 5V to a GP pin, never a power pin to GND, power off at SW5 before changing wires.
