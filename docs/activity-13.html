📌 Pin reference

🎯 Your mission

Get every sensor on your LilEx5 onto one live web page — through a cloud program you deployed, into a spreadsheet with your name on it.

Five parts, and only one of them is Python. You will put a small program in the cloud, tell your board where it lives, run it, open the dashboard, and then — the part none of the earlier activities could do — scroll back and look at what the board recorded before you got there.

What you will learn

📤 POST

The other way to send. The board knocks on a web address, hands over its readings and waits to be told they arrived.

🏷️ Readings with names

One message carrying many values, each labelled. The label is what decides which column it lands in.

📋 A store of your own

Every reading becomes a row in a spreadsheet you can open, sort, chart and download.

🕐 History

A gauge shows you now. A sheet shows you last Tuesday — and that is the difference between a display and data.

Today you write almost no Python, and that is the lesson Every activity so far put the difficulty in the program. Today the program is handed to you and the work moves somewhere else entirely: deploying a service, keeping an address safe, and deciding how often to measure. That is what building an IoT system actually feels like once the board works.
What you are reusing today, and will not be taught again Joining WiFi and the wait that ends by itself (Activity 10), the I²C sensor bus (Activity 7 and Activity 8), the OLED screen (Activity 7), and the idea of a service in the middle (Activity 10 and Activity 11).

🏠 The middle is yours this time — the one new idea

Activity 10 sent a reading to Adafruit IO. Activity 11 took an order back from it. Both times the middle belonged to somebody else.

Adafruit IO is a good service and it is free, and none of that is the problem. The problem is everything you could not do with it: you could not keep more than a few days of readings, you could not add a reading it had no box for, you could not open the raw numbers in a spreadsheet, and if the account went away so did everything in it. You were a guest.

Owning it costs something, and the page is not going to pretend otherwise A service you run is a service you have to look after. You need a Google account. You have to keep the address safe. If the deployment is deleted the board keeps sending into nothing and nobody tells it. And Apps Script is slower than the broker in Activity 10 — a few hundred milliseconds per reading rather than a few. You are trading convenience for control, deliberately.

🔄 How the data goes round

Activity 10 published and walked away. Today the board asks, and something answers.

In Activity 10 the board handed a message to a broker and carried on without ever finding out what happened to it. That is publishing: fast, cheap, and completely silent about whether anybody was listening. Today is the other shape. The board opens a connection to a web address, sends its readings, and waits for a reply. The reply is short — it is basically the word yes — but it is there, and it means the board finds out immediately when something is wrong.

Both shapes are real and both are used Publishing suits a sensor that reports constantly and does not care — thousands of readings a minute, losing one is fine. Posting suits anything that has to be recorded properly. Most real systems do both, for different jobs, and now you have written one of each.

🏷️ A reading with its name on it

Activity 10 sent one bare number to a feed that already knew what it meant. Today one message carries a dozen readings at once, so every one of them has to say what it is.

The board packs them into a shape called JSON: a list of name → value pairs, wrapped in curly brackets. You will see this shape for the rest of your life — it is how nearly everything on the internet hands data to everything else.

🧩 What you need

This activity has two halves, and they need different things. Read both lists before you start — you can do the second half with nothing but a browser.

The board half

A real LilEx5 and a network. There is no Wokwi route for this one: the program reads the whole sensor set that is soldered to this board, and the simulator has none of it. If you do not have a board today, pair up with somebody who does — then do the cloud half on your own machine, because that half is yours to keep.

  • The LilEx5, a USB cable, and Thonny — as in every activity since Activity 0
  • 2.4 GHz WiFi that actually lets new devices on. A Pico W cannot join 5 GHz at all, and a school network with a sign-in page defeats it completely. A phone hotspot on 2.4 GHz is a perfectly good answer and not a workaround — the same warning as Activity 10
  • The Activity 13 file pack from your teacher

The cloud half

  • A Google account. Your school one is fine if it can reach Apps Script — some cannot, and your teacher will have checked
  • A browser. That is all

☁️ Part 1 — put the script in the cloud

This is the part that makes the middle exist. Doing it once gives you a web address that anything, anywhere, can send readings to.

Your teacher may have done this already If you have been handed a /exec address, skip to Part 2 and use it. Come back and do this yourself afterwards if you want a sheet that is yours rather than the class’s.
1

Make the spreadsheet first

Go to sheets.new and give it a name you will recognise — LilEx5 readings will do. Leave it empty. The script writes the header row itself the first time a reading arrives.

2

Open the script editor from inside the sheet

Extensions → Apps Script. Starting from inside the sheet is what joins the two together — a script made any other way has no idea which spreadsheet you mean.

3

Paste the program in

Delete the few starter lines that are already there, and paste in the whole of LilEx5_AppsScript.js from your file pack. Save.

4

Deploy it as a web app

Deploy → New deployment, then set the three things in the picture below. All three matter, and two of them are the ones people get wrong.

5

Copy the address it gives you

The last screen shows a Web app URL. Copy it somewhere you can get at it twice — it goes into your board in Part 2 and into your dashboard in Part 4, and it must be exactly the same in both.

Treat that address like a password, because it is one You just set Who has access to Anyone, and you had to — the board has no way to sign in. So the address is the only thing standing between your sheet and the rest of the internet. Do not post it in a group chat, do not put it in a file you upload anywhere, and do not paste it into a screenshot. If it does get out, deploy again: a new deployment gets a new address and the old one stops being useful.

⚙️ Part 2 — tell the board where to send

Four lines at the bottom of config.py. This is the only file you edit today, and everybody’s is different.

Your WiFi, your password, your address The network name and password are yours — whatever your own phone or laptop is on. The /exec address is the one from Part 1. Keep the quotation marks: everything between them is text, and the board will try to join a network literally called YourNetworkName if you leave it there.

🔍 What each line is for

Code is shown as a picture. The name of the network, spelled exactly as it appears on your phone. Capital letters count.
Code is shown as a picture. Its password. If the network has none, leave the quotes empty: "".
Code is shown as a picture. Where to send. The whole address, ending in /exec, between quotes.
Code is shown as a picture. How long to wait between readings, in milliseconds. 5000 is five seconds. This one number decides how much of your day ends up in the sheet.
Milliseconds, not seconds sleep(5) in Activity 2 meant five seconds. REPORT_INTERVAL_MS set to 5000 means the same five seconds, counted a thousand times finer. Put 5 in there by mistake and the board will try to send two hundred readings a second, which will fail in a way that looks like a broken network.

▶️ Part 3 — upload the pack and run it

In Thonny, open the Files panel (View → Files). Right-click each file on your computer’s side and choose Upload to /. Then open main_dashboard.py and press F5.

Your old main.py can stay main_dashboard.py is a separate program you start by hand. Nothing you built in the earlier activities is overwritten, and the board still does what it did before when you unplug it and plug it back in.

The OLED screen carries on working while this happens — press the buttons and browse the sensor pages exactly as in Activity 8. The board is now showing you the readings and sending them at the same time, which is the first time in this module that it has done two useful jobs at once.

Go and look at the sheet Open your spreadsheet in a browser tab. The first reading created a header row out of the names in the message; every one after it is another line underneath. Watch it grow. This is the moment the activity is actually about — the graphs later are just a nicer way of looking at these rows.

📊 Part 4 — open the dashboard

The rows are the data. The dashboard is a nicer way of looking at them — and it is a single file that lives on your computer, not a website you log in to.

1

Open LilEx5_Dashboard.html in a text editor

Thonny will do, so will Notepad. Not a browser yet.

2

Paste your address into the top of the file

The first thing in the file is a short block of settings. Put your /exec address between the quotes after API_URL, and save.

3

Now open the same file in a browser

Double-click it. Within a few seconds the status turns green and the cards start filling.

Code is shown as a picture — turn on JavaScript to see it.
Three settings, and you only have to change the first REFRESH_INTERVAL is how often the page asks for new rows, and it has nothing to do with how often the board sends them — those are two separate clocks and they do not have to agree. HISTORY_ON_OPEN is how many past rows to load when the page opens, and it is the one the exercise is about.

What you should see: a card for every reading the board sends — temperature, humidity, pressure, air quality, proximity, compass heading, the accelerometer and gyroscope in three axes each, the magnetometer, and the four analogue channels — and underneath them, graphs of the ones that are worth watching move.

🎉 That is a complete IoT system, and every piece of it is yours. A sensor, a network, a service, a store and a display — the same five pieces a weather station or a factory monitor is built from. Open the dashboard on your phone, on the same WiFi or not: the readings are coming from Google, not from your board, so it works from anywhere.

🕐 Part 5 — look at yesterday

Here is the thing none of the earlier activities could do.

Stop the program on the board. Close the dashboard. Go away. Come back tomorrow, open LilEx5_Dashboard_History.html instead, and the graphs are already full — of readings taken while nobody was watching.

The history view needs one extra piece in your Apps Script — the file apps-script-history.gs in your pack, pasted onto the end of what is already there and deployed again. Your teacher has the two-line instruction that goes with it. Without it the page still works; it just starts with empty graphs and fills them as it goes.

How much history is there? As much as the board has sent. At one reading every five seconds a single school day is about six thousand rows, and a spreadsheet holds ten million cells — so the sheet fills up eventually, and how fast is entirely decided by that one number in config.py. That is the exercise.

🛠️ If it is not working

Almost always one of these, and almost always in this order.

What you seeTry this
[WiFi] Connection timeoutCheck WIFI_SSID and WIFI_PASS for a capital letter in the wrong place. If they are right, the network is the problem: a Pico W cannot join 5 GHz, and it cannot get past a sign-in page. Use a phone hotspot on 2.4 GHz.
An IP address appears, then nothing elseThe network is fine and the posting is failing. Open your /exec address in a browser tab on the same network — if the page does not load there, the board cannot reach it either.
Google says “authorisation required”You have not run the deployment yourself yet. Open the /exec address in your own browser once and authorise it. The board can post to it from then on.
The sheet stays emptyIs it the right sheet? A script made from File → New instead of Extensions → Apps Script writes into a different spreadsheet, or none at all. Start again from inside the sheet.
The dashboard says no dataNormal for the first ten seconds — the sheet has a header row and nothing under it yet. If it lasts longer, the address in the dashboard and the address in config.py are not the same one. Compare them character by character; re-deploying makes a new address and the old one keeps working for the board while the page reads nothing.
Rows arrive but the dashboard cards stay emptyA name is spelled differently at the two ends. Look at the header row: an unexpected extra column is the giveaway.
It worked, and now it has stoppedDid you edit the Apps Script and forget to deploy again? Saving changes the code; only deploying changes what the address serves.
OSError: -2 or ECONNABORTEDThe board lost the network mid-send. It will usually pick itself up on the next reading. If it does not, stop and run again.
The program will not stopIt loops for ever, like every program since Activity 2. Thonny’s Stop, or Ctrl+C in the Shell.
The whole board went deadYou knocked SW5. Still the power switch.

🚀 Exercise — a whole day on one screen

Five seconds is far too often for something you want to look back at. Fix that, at both ends.

Task: make the board record one reading a minute instead of one every five seconds, and make the dashboard open showing a whole school day of them.

  • Two numbers change, in two different files. Neither is Python you have to work out — both are lines you have already edited today
  • Work out the second number rather than guessing it. A school day is about seven hours
  • Nothing about the board, the sensors or the script changes at all

What success looks like

💡 Clue 1 — the board’s number

It is REPORT_INTERVAL_MS in config.py, and the trap is the unit. That MS is not decoration: the number is in milliseconds, so a second is a thousand of them.

You want one reading a minute. Work out how many milliseconds a minute is, and put that number in. Save the file, upload it to the Pico again, and run. The Shell tells you straight away whether you got it right — count how long you wait between lines.

💡 Clue 2 — the dashboard’s number

HISTORY_ON_OPEN is a count of rows, not a length of time. The page has no idea how far apart your readings are; it just asks for the last n of them.

So you have to do the joining up yourself. One reading a minute means one row a minute. How many minutes are there in a seven-hour school day? That is your number.

Notice what this means: if you later go back to a reading every five seconds without touching the dashboard, the same number stops being a day and becomes about thirty-five minutes. The two settings are tied together by arithmetic and by nothing else.

💡 Clue 3 — nearly there

Five things to check before you decide it is broken:

  • Is the interval in milliseconds? 60 is not a minute; it is sixteen readings a second.
  • Did you upload the edited config.py to the Pico? Editing it on your computer changes nothing until it goes across.
  • Did you stop and run the program again? It reads the settings once, at the start.
  • Did you save the dashboard file before reopening it in the browser? And did the browser reload rather than show you the old page — try a hard refresh.
  • Does your sheet actually have a day of rows yet? Asking for four hundred when there are nine gives you nine, and that is not a fault.

Check it by leaving the board running while you do something else for ten minutes, then counting the rows. Ten minutes should be ten rows.

No answer on this page There is no answer block here on purpose — the arithmetic is the exercise, and the Shell will tell you within two minutes whether you have it. Your teacher has the worked answer if the class needs it.

🌟 Going further

Finished early? Try these — no answers given.

  1. Make a chart inside the spreadsheet itself. Select the temperature column and insert a chart. You did not need the dashboard for that at all — which is worth thinking about.
  2. Work out when your sheet will fill up. A spreadsheet holds ten million cells. Count your columns, count your rows per day, and find the date. Then decide what you would do about it in a system that has to run for a year.
  3. Put the dashboard somewhere other people can open it. It is one HTML file with nothing else attached — the same GitHub Pages that serves this website will serve it. Then think hard about the address inside it before you do.
  4. Send a reading of your own. Add the soil percentage from Activity 9 to the message in main_dashboard.py and watch a new column appear in the sheet with no other change anywhere.
  5. Compare two boards. Point a second board at the same address, add a name to each message, and you have a class weather network. Working out how to tell the two apart in one sheet is the real task.
  6. Make the board survive the WiFi going away — keep the readings it could not send and post them when the network comes back. That one line of thinking is most of what makes real IoT hard.

📖 Words to remember

HTTPThe rules browsers and web addresses talk by. Your board used them today, exactly as a browser does.
POSTSending data to a web address and waiting to be told what happened to it. The opposite of publishing and walking away.
EndpointA web address that a program, rather than a person, is meant to visit. Yours ends in /exec.
DeployPutting a program somewhere it can be reached, and getting an address for it. Saving is not deploying.
JSONA message made of name-and-value pairs in curly brackets. The commonest way data moves on the internet.
Header rowThe first row of the sheet, holding the names. It is written once, from the first message that arrives, and everything after it lines up underneath.
HistoryReadings from before you were looking. The reason a store is worth more than a display.
Sampling intervalHow long the board waits between readings. It decides how much detail you keep and how fast the sheet fills.
QuotaA limit the service you are using puts on how much you may do. Everybody has one — Adafruit IO’s was 30 a minute, Google’s is generous but real.
